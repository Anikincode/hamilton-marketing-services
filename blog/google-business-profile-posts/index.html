Google Business Profile posts: what to post, how often, and what to skip

Posts are free space on your own Google listing, and most businesses leave it empty. Here's a structure that's quick to write, honest, and points people at the right page.

Kirill Anikin · October 30, 2026 · 6 min read

Key takeaways

  • Posts are free space on your own Google listing. Use them to send people to a specific service page.
  • Use one fixed structure: a service-and-area headline, a short brand line, three short paragraphs and a button.
  • Rotate services and areas so no two posts in a row say the same thing.
  • Use real photos, put the link in the button, and keep phone numbers out of the text.
  • Never invent an offer. Posts don't replace good categories or reviews. They support them.

Open your Google Business Profile and look at your posts. If the newest one is from last spring, you're in good company. And you're leaving a free billboard blank.

Google Business Profile posts are short updates that appear on your listing in Google Search and Maps. They're one of the few things on your profile you fully control, and they're easy to neglect.

What Google Business Profile posts are

A post is a short message with an optional photo or video and a button. Google offers a few types:

  • Updates: general news, a service you want to highlight, a recent job.
  • Offers: a real promotion, with dates and terms.
  • Events: something happening on a set date.

Buttons let people act straight from the post: Learn more, Book, Call now and a few others. That button is the part that matters most, because it's how a post turns into a visit or a call.

What posts can and can't do

Let's be straight about this, because a lot of people oversell posts.

Posts won't fix a wrong category, a thin website or a lack of reviews. We don't sell them as a ranking trick, and you shouldn't buy them as one.

What they do well is simpler:

  • They show searchers that a real, active business is behind the listing.
  • They let you put a specific service in front of people, like "furnace repair in Stoney Creek", with a button straight to that page.
  • They give you a regular reason to mention the areas you serve, in plain language.

Think of posts as the chalkboard outside a café. The board doesn't make the coffee better. It tells people walking past what's on today.

The structure we use for every post

Writer's block kills posting schedules. A fixed structure fixes that. Every post we write follows the same four parts, with the wording changed every time:

  1. Headline: service plus area. "Furnace repair in Stoney Creek." "Lawn care in Waterdown." Always both.
  2. Brand line. One short sentence about what makes you different, worded a little differently each time.
  3. Three short paragraphs. The customer's problem, what you do about it, and a local line that names the area and the outcome.
  4. The button, pointing at the matching service page on your website.

Here's what that looks like for a made-up heating company:

Furnace repair on Hamilton Mountain

Warm house, straight answers.

A furnace that quits on the first cold night is never convenient. If yours is short-cycling, blowing cold air or making a new noise, it's worth a look before it stops completely.

We diagnose the problem, explain the options in plain English and give you a price before any work starts.

We work across the Mountain, from Upper James to Upper Wentworth, so local customers on the Mountain aren't left waiting on a crew from across the city.

Then the Learn more button goes to the furnace repair page. Notice what isn't there: no phone number in the text, no invented discount, no claim the business can't prove.

How often should you post?

Consistency beats bursts. A pile of posts in one afternoon and then nothing for months tells a searcher less than a steady trickle.

For our clients, we schedule daily Business Profile posts. We write a month at a time, review them, and load them into Local Viking, a tool that publishes one post a day on schedule. That's a system built for an agency.

If you're doing it yourself, pick a pace you can keep. Weekly is a sensible start for most owners. Put it in your calendar like any other job.

Rotate services and areas

The fastest way to make posts useless is to write the same one over and over. So rotate.

Make a simple grid: your services down one side, your areas across the top. Then work through it so the same service and area don't come up again within the same week.

DayServiceArea
MondayDrain cleaningStoney Creek
TuesdayWater heater repairAncaster
WednesdayEmergency plumbingHamilton Mountain
ThursdaySump pump installationDundas
FridayDrain cleaningWaterdown

Only post about services that have a live page on your site. If there's no sump pump page yet, there's no sump pump post yet. A button that leads to a missing page wastes the one click you earned.

If you're unsure which services to build pages for, start with your Business Profile categories, then follow our guide to service pages that rank.

Images: real first, stock only if you must

Every post should have an image. A post with a photo simply looks more alive.

  • Use your own photos first. A real crew, a real van, a real finished job in a real Hamilton driveway.
  • If you have nothing for that service yet, a stock photo is better than nothing. Choose one with no logos, no text, no phone numbers and nothing that looks obviously like another country.
  • Match the image to the service. A drain post gets a drain photo, not a smiling handshake.
  • Never repeat an image in the same month, and keep a note of which ones are stock so you can swap in real job photos later.

The rules that keep posts live

Google reviews posts, and some get rejected. These habits keep yours out of trouble.

  • Keep phone numbers out of the text. Google's own help says posts with phone numbers in them may be rejected. Use the Call button instead.
  • Put the link in the button. The button is what people tap. Don't count on a web address typed into the text.
  • Never invent an offer. Use an offer post only for a promotion you're really running, with real terms.
  • No spun text. Tools that automatically shuffle words produce near-copies. Write each post on purpose.
  • Check that they went live. A day or two after the first posts of a batch, open your profile and confirm they're showing. If one wasn't approved, find out why before the rest go out with the same problem.

Posts are one part of the profile

Posts work best on a profile that's already right: correct categories, real services, steady reviews, accurate hours. On a weak profile, posting is decorating a house with a cracked foundation.

FAQ

Do Google Business Profile posts help rankings?

We don't promise that they move rankings on their own, and you should be wary of anyone who does. What they reliably do is keep your listing current and send interested people to the right service page with one tap.

How often should I post on my Google Business Profile?

As often as you can keep up with. We schedule daily posts for clients. For an owner doing it alone, weekly is a realistic start. A steady rhythm matters more than the exact number.

What should a post link to?

The service page that matches the post. A post about drain cleaning in Stoney Creek should send people to your drain cleaning page, not your homepage.

Can I use AI to write my posts?

As a first draft, sure. Then check every claim, make each post genuinely different, and add the local detail only you know. An AI tool doesn't know what you did on a job in Dundas last week.
