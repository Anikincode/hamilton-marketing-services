Discovered – currently not indexed: why Google is skipping your pages

Google knows your pages exist. It just hasn't got round to reading them. Here's why that happens on small business sites, and what gets them moving.

Kirill Anikin · October 30, 2026 · 5 min read

Key takeaways

  • "Discovered – currently not indexed" means Google found the page's address but hasn't crawled it yet. It's not a penalty.
  • We audited a site where Google had indexed about 5 of its 48 pages. The rest sat in this status.
  • On a small site, the usual causes are pages that look alike, pages that look thin, and pages nothing important links to.
  • Fewer, better, well-linked pages fix it. Hitting "Request indexing" over and over doesn't.

Imagine printing 48 flyers, handing them all out, and finding out about 5 got read.

That's roughly what one site we audited was going through. Google had indexed about 5 of its 48 pages. Search Console listed the rest as "Discovered – currently not indexed".

If you've seen that phrase in your own Search Console, here's what it means and what to do about it.

What "Discovered – currently not indexed" means

Google works in stages. First it discovers a page, usually from your sitemap or a link. Then it crawls the page, meaning it visits and reads it. Then it decides whether to index it, meaning add it to the catalogue of pages it can show in results.

"Discovered – currently not indexed" means the page is stuck at stage one. Google has the address. It hasn't visited yet.

It's easy to confuse with a neighbouring status, so here's the difference:

Status in Search ConsoleWhat Google has doneWhat it usually tells you
Discovered – currently not indexedFound the address, hasn't read the pageGoogle doesn't see enough reason to prioritize it yet
Crawled – currently not indexedRead the page and decided not to list it, for nowGoogle read it and wasn't convinced it adds enough
IndexedRead it and listed itThe page can now compete in search results

You'll find these under Indexing, then Pages, in Search Console. Google's help page on the Page indexing report lists every status.

Why it happens on small local sites

Google's own explanation of this status talks about rescheduling crawls so it doesn't overload your server. That can matter on a huge site. A small local business website rarely has that problem.

So on a local site, look at priority instead. Google is deciding which pages are worth its time, and these are the usual reasons it decides "not yet".

The pages look alike

This is the big one. A site has a stack of area pages, "Plumber in Stoney Creek", "Plumber in Ancaster", "Plumber in Dundas", where only the town name changes. Google may read a few, spot the pattern, and find little reason to read the rest.

The pages look thin

A page with a heading, two sentences and a contact form doesn't give Google much to go on. If the pages it has already read on your site were thin, it has less reason to rush to the next ones.

Nothing important links to them

Pages that only appear in your sitemap, and aren't linked from your homepage, menu or other pages, look unimportant. If you don't link to a page, why should Google think it matters?

The site hasn't earned much trust yet

A new domain with few links and little history can be crawled more cautiously. That changes as the site earns links, mentions and visitors.

Something else is wrong

Before you go further, rule out a technical block. A robots.txt rule or a noindex tag shows up as a different status, but it's worth a quick check while you're there. See robots.txt mistakes.

How to fix it, in order

  1. Decide which pages deserve to exist. Be honest. If a stack of area pages all say the same thing, merge them into a smaller number of real ones and redirect the rest. Fewer, better pages beat a pile of copies.
  2. Make each remaining page genuinely different. A Waterdown page should talk about work in Waterdown: the jobs you do there, the questions customers there ask. Our guide to service pages that rank shows what belongs on the page, and information gain explains why saying something new matters.
  3. Link to them from pages Google already values. Your homepage, your main menu and your related service pages. A "furnace repair Ancaster" page should be reachable from your furnace repair page, not just the sitemap.
  4. Keep your sitemap clean. List only the pages you want indexed. Leave out thank-you pages, test pages and redirected addresses.
  5. Request indexing for a few key pages. In URL Inspection, request indexing for your most important pages once they're improved. It's a nudge, not a fix, and Google limits how often you can use it.
  6. Earn some outside signals. Link the right page from your Business Profile, get listed in reputable local directories, and earn a few real links. They all tell Google your site is worth its time.

What not to do

  • Don't resubmit your sitemap every day. Google already has it.
  • Don't pay for "instant indexing" services. They don't fix why Google skipped the pages.
  • Don't publish more pages to make up for it. More thin pages make the problem worse, not better.
  • Don't delete pages without a redirect. If a page had any links or rankings, send its address to the closest matching page.

How long until pages get indexed?

There's no fixed timeline, and anyone who gives you one is guessing. After you've made the changes, check the Pages report every week or two. You're looking for the "Discovered" count to fall and the indexed count to rise.

If the improved pages move to "Crawled – currently not indexed", that's progress of a sort. Google has read them. It's now telling you it wants more from the content itself.

Frequently asked questions

Is "Discovered – currently not indexed" a penalty?

No. It's a status, not a punishment. Google has found the page and hasn't crawled it yet. Nothing has been taken away from your site.

Will clicking "Request indexing" fix it?

Sometimes for one page, briefly. But it doesn't change why Google skipped the page. If the page is thin, a copy of others or poorly linked, fix that first, then request indexing.

Should I delete the pages that aren't indexed?

Delete or merge the ones that don't deserve to exist, and redirect their addresses. Improve the ones that do. Don't delete a page that's simply new and needs time.

Does this hurt the pages that are already indexed?

Not directly. But a site full of thin, look-alike pages can give Google a weaker impression overall, so cleaning them up can help the whole site.
