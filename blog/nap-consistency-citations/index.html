NAP consistency: why your name, address and phone must match everywhere

Google checks what the rest of the web says about your business. When your listings disagree with each other, you make it harder for Google, and customers, to trust any of them.

Kirill Anikin · October 30, 2026 · 5 min read

Key takeaways

  • NAP stands for name, address and phone. Citations are the places your business is listed online.
  • Google uses information from across the web, including directories, to judge how well known a business is.
  • The big problems are old addresses, old phone numbers, duplicate listings and phone numbers shared with another business. Small formatting differences matter far less.
  • Write one master version of your details, then make every listing match it.
  • Service-area businesses keep the name, phone and area consistent, and keep the hidden address hidden everywhere.

Imagine three neighbours giving a taxi driver three different addresses for the same house. The driver might get there. Or might give up and take the next fare.

That's what inconsistent listings do to Google. Your business is the house, and every directory, social profile and map listing is a neighbour giving directions.

What is NAP consistency?

NAP is short for name, address, phone. NAP consistency means those three details read the same everywhere your business appears online.

Each of those appearances is called a citation: your Google Business Profile, your website footer, Bing, Apple Maps, Facebook, Yelp, industry directories, your local chamber of commerce listing, and so on.

For a service-area business that hides its address, think of it as name, phone and area instead. The principle is the same.

Why it matters

Google says prominence, one of the three things behind local rankings, is based partly on information it finds about a business across the web, including directories. If those sources agree, they back each other up. If they disagree, Google has to decide which one to believe.

There's a plainer reason too. Customers use these listings. A homeowner in Dundas who finds your old number on a directory and gets a "not in service" message doesn't try again. They call the next company.

And AI assistants that answer "who should I call?" pull from the same web. Conflicting details make your business harder to describe with confidence.

What inconsistency really looks like

It's rarely dramatic. It's usually leftovers from a business that changed over time.

  • An old address after a move. You moved from Dundas to Ancaster a while ago, and plenty of directories still have you in Dundas.
  • An old phone number from before you switched providers, or a former partner's cell.
  • Two names. Your legal name on one listing, your trade name on another, and a shortened version on a third.
  • Duplicate listings. Two Google profiles for the same business, often one created years ago by someone who's long gone.
  • A shared phone number. We audited two separate businesses that shared one phone number across their listings. That leaves Google guessing which business is which.
  • The wrong city on your own website. We've found text left over from a template naming a different Ontario city on several service pages. Your own site is a citation too.

The Hamilton detail: which place name?

Hamilton has an extra wrinkle. Since amalgamation in 2001, Stoney Creek, Ancaster, Dundas, Flamborough (including Waterdown) and Glanbrook (including Binbrook) have all been part of the City of Hamilton.

In practice, some addresses get written with the community name, like "Stoney Creek, ON", and others with "Hamilton, ON". Neither is wrong as such. The problem is using both for the same business.

So pick one form. Use the one that matches your Google Business Profile, and copy it everywhere, exactly.

The same goes for your phone number. Hamilton uses the 905, 289 and 365 area codes. Whichever number you use, it should be the same number on every listing.

Does "St." versus "Street" matter?

Much less than people fear. Google is good at understanding that "Upper James St." and "Upper James Street" are the same street.

Spend your energy on the differences that can actually mislead someone: a wrong number, an old address, a different business name, a missing suite number. Then tidy the formatting if you have time.

How to find and fix your NAP problems

  1. Write your master NAP. One line each for name, address (or service area) and phone, exactly as they appear on your Google Business Profile. This is the version everything copies.
  2. Search your phone number in quotes on Google. Note every listing that comes up, and anything that shouldn't.
  3. Search your old details. Your old address, old phone number and any old business names. These find the listings that need updating.
  4. Check the main listings by hand. Google Business Profile, Bing Places for Business, Apple Business Connect, your Facebook page, Yelp, and any industry or local directories you belong to.
  5. Log it in a spreadsheet. Columns for the site, the listing link, the name, address and phone as shown, and whether it's fixed.
  6. Fix the worst first. Wrong phone numbers and old addresses before abbreviations. Duplicates before anything cosmetic.
  7. Recheck in a few weeks. Some directories are slow to update, and some copy their data from others.

A tool can speed this up. We use BrightLocal to track citations for clients, because it finds listings you'd never think to search for. For a small business, the manual steps above get you most of the way.

Your website is the anchor

Of all your citations, your website is the one you fully control. Make it the cleanest:

  • The same name, phone and address (or area) in the footer of every page
  • A contact page that matches your Business Profile exactly
  • Structured data with the same details, so machines read the same thing people do

Our schema starter pack has copy-ready LocalBusiness markup that keeps your details consistent in code too.

Service-area businesses: keep the hidden address hidden

If you hide your address on Google because you work from home, don't undo that elsewhere. Some directories ask for an address. Where they offer a way to hide it, use it. Where they don't, think about whether you need that listing at all.

Your consistent details become your business name, your phone number and the areas you serve. More on the setup in service-area businesses on Google Maps.

Keep it consistent

NAP consistency isn't a one-time cleanup. People suggest edits to listings, directories merge data, and a staff member creates "a quick new profile" without telling anyone.

Protecting what you've already built is what we call defensive SEO. Our article on defensive SEO covers the other ways rankings get quietly undone.

FAQ

What does NAP stand for in SEO?

Name, address and phone number. NAP consistency means those details match everywhere your business is listed: your Business Profile, website, directories and social profiles.

Do I need hundreds of citations?

No. A smaller number of accurate listings on the sites your customers actually use beats a long list of low-quality directories. Get the main ones right first, then add relevant industry and local listings.

I moved. What should I update first?

Your Google Business Profile and your website, then Bing, Apple Maps and Facebook, then everything else. Search your old address afterwards to catch the listings you missed.

What if I find a duplicate Google profile?

Don't just ignore it. Duplicates split your reviews and confuse Google. If you can access it, follow Google's steps for duplicates. If you can't, report it as a duplicate from the listing.
