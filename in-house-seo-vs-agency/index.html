In-house SEO vs agency: which fits a small business?

Hiring your own SEO person feels like taking control. Sometimes it is. Sometimes it's paying a full salary for a part-time job. Here's how to tell which one you're looking at.

Kirill Anikin · October 30, 2026 · 5 min read

Key takeaways

  • In-house SEO costs more than a salary: add payroll costs, tools, training and the time you spend managing.
  • An agency spreads its specialists and tools across many clients, so you pay for a share rather than a whole person.
  • In-house wins on knowing your business. An agency wins on range of skills and on seeing what works across many sites.
  • For many small businesses, a hybrid works best: someone in-house handles reviews and photos, and an outside expert sets the plan.

Picture a busy HVAC company in Stoney Creek. The owner hires a marketing person to "do the SEO". A few months later, that person is also running social media, answering phones and fixing the printer.

It's an easy trap to fall into, and it's nobody's fault. SEO for one small business often isn't a full-time job. That's the heart of this decision.

Your real options

  1. Hire in-house. An employee whose job includes SEO.
  2. Hire an agency. A company that does the work for a monthly fee.
  3. Do it yourself, with outside help. You or a staff member do the doing, and a consultant sets the plan and checks the work.

If you're leaning towards the third option, our guide on whether you can do SEO yourself covers what an owner can handle alone.

What in-house SEO really costs

The salary is the number everyone looks at. It's only the start.

ToolsYour own subscriptions for keyword research, rank tracking and site crawlingOften included, but ask
TrainingCourses, and time to keep up as search changesThe agency's problem
ManagementYour hours setting goals and checking workFewer hours, but still some
Skill gapsFreelancers for the skills your hire doesn't haveCovered, if the agency has those skills
TurnoverHiring and training again if they leaveSwitching agencies, if it comes to that

Then there's the skills problem. Local SEO touches your Business Profile, technical fixes, writing, web development, tracking and sometimes ads. It's rare for one person to be strong at all of it.

A simple way to compare the costs

Use your own numbers, and add up a full year of each:

  • In-house = salary + payroll costs + tools + training + (your management hours × what an hour of your time is worth)
  • Agency = a year of monthly fees + (your management hours × what an hour of your time is worth)

Then ask the bigger question: how much SEO work does your business actually have? A one-truck appliance repair business in Hamilton might need a burst of work up front and light upkeep after. A company with several locations, from Burlington to Grimsby, might genuinely fill a full-time role.

Spotting patterns matters more than it sounds. When we audit local-business websites, the same problems come up again and again: missing headings, wrong cities in titles, template text left over from another business. When we scored 35 live sites on Sep 20, 2026, the average was 34 out of 100. Someone who has fixed a problem on many sites finds it on yours quickly.

When in-house makes sense

  • You have several locations or a large service area, with enough pages and profiles to fill a role.
  • You publish a lot: new projects, seasonal offers, regular updates.
  • You already have a web or marketing team for the hire to work with.

Example: a renovation company with crews in Ancaster, Waterdown and Burlington, each with its own Business Profile and a steady stream of finished projects to write up.

When an agency makes more sense

  • You're a small team, and nobody has time to manage an SEO hire.
  • You need several skills, not one.
  • The work is heavy up front and lighter after, like a site cleanup followed by maintenance.

Example: a small plumbing business on the Mountain that needs its site fixed, a page per service and a steady flow of reviews, but not a full-time SEO person.

The hybrid that often works best

For a lot of small businesses, the best answer is a split. Someone in-house, often an office manager or coordinator, handles the parts that need to be close to customers: asking for reviews, taking job photos and answering questions about services.

An outside expert handles the rest: strategy, technical fixes, page planning and monthly checks. That's what an SEO consultant arrangement is built for. If you want the doing handled too, see what an SEO company does, month by month.

Questions to ask yourself before you decide

Before you post a job ad or sign with anyone, answer these honestly. They'll usually point you to the right option.

  • How much work is there, really? List your services and the areas you serve. A furnace company covering the Mountain, Stoney Creek and Ancaster has more pages to build and track than a single-service business in Dundas.
  • Is the work steady, or front-loaded? If most of it is a one-time cleanup, a full-time hire will run out of things to do.
  • Who would manage an SEO hire? If the answer is "me, in the evenings", that's a real cost.
  • Which skills do you need most? Writing, technical fixes and Business Profile work are different jobs. Know which one matters most for you right now.
  • What happens if they leave? Make sure logins, notes and content would stay with the business either way.

Common questions about in-house SEO vs an agency

Can I start with an agency and bring SEO in-house later?

Yes, and it's a sensible path. Make sure every account and every page written for you is in your name from day one, and ask for written notes on what was done. That makes the handover simple.

What should an in-house SEO hire know?

At minimum: Google Business Profile, Search Console, page titles and headings, redirects, and how to write a service page. Ask them how they'd find out why a page isn't indexed. Their answer tells you a lot.

Will an agency understand my business as well as an employee?

Not on day one. A good agency closes the gap by asking lots of questions and having you approve everything it writes. Your knowledge of the trade is the raw material.

Who owns the work if I switch?

You should. Your website, domain, Business Profile, Search Console and every page written for you should stay in your name, whoever does the work.
