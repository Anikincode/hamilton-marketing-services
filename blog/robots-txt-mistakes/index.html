Robots.txt mistakes that can hide your whole website from Google

It's a tiny text file most owners have never opened. Get one line wrong and Google stops reading your site.

Kirill Anikin · October 30, 2026 · 6 min read

Key takeaways

  • Robots.txt tells search engines which parts of your site they may crawl. Two short lines can block the whole thing.
  • We've audited a live business site whose robots.txt blocked all indexing. It looked completely normal in a browser.
  • Robots.txt doesn't remove pages from Google. To keep a page out of results, use a noindex tag instead.
  • You can check yours in a couple of minutes: open the file in your browser, then look at Search Console's robots.txt report.

Imagine a "Closed" sign hanging on your shop door that only Google can see. Customers walk in fine. Google reads the sign, turns around and leaves.

That's what a bad robots.txt file does. And because nobody looks at it, it can sit there for months.

We've seen it on a live local-business website: a robots.txt that blocked all indexing, so Google was told to ignore the entire site. The owner was paying for marketing while the front door was locked.

What robots.txt actually is

Robots.txt is a plain text file that sits at the root of your website, at yourdomain.ca/robots.txt. Search engines read it before they crawl anything else.

It's a set of house rules. Each block names a crawler (the "user-agent") and lists the folders it may or may not visit. Here's what the common lines mean:

Line in the fileWhat it means in plain English
User-agent: *The rules below apply to every crawler.
Disallow: /Don't crawl anything on this site. This is the line that hides everything.
Disallow: (with nothing after it)Nothing is off limits. Crawl everything.
Disallow: /quote-thanks/Stay out of this one folder or page.
Sitemap: https://yourdomain.ca/sitemap.xmlHere's the list of pages we want you to find.

Notice how close the second and third rows are. One forward slash is the difference between "crawl everything" and "crawl nothing".

Google's own introduction to robots.txt is worth a read if you want the full rules.

Mistake 1: Blocking the whole site

This is the big one, and it nearly always happens by accident.

While a new site is being built, it's sensible to keep it out of Google. Developers add a block, or tick a "hide from search engines" setting. Then the site launches, everyone celebrates, and nobody removes the block.

Picture a Binbrook landscaper whose new site goes live in March. The photos are great, the service pages are written, and by June it still hasn't appeared for a single search. Nothing is wrong with the content. Google simply hasn't been allowed in.

WordPress has a setting called "Discourage search engines from indexing this site" for exactly this stage. It's useful during a build. It's a disaster if it stays ticked. It's one of the first things we check after any website redesign.

Mistake 2: Using robots.txt to hide a page

Lots of owners think robots.txt removes a page from Google. It doesn't.

Robots.txt controls crawling, meaning whether Google reads the page. It doesn't control indexing, meaning whether Google lists it. If other pages link to a blocked URL, Google can still show that address in results, usually with no description, because it was never allowed to read what's on it.

If you want a page kept out of search, like the thank-you page after your quote form, use a "noindex" tag on the page. And here's the catch: Google has to be able to crawl the page to see that tag. Block it in robots.txt and the noindex is never read.

Mistake 3: Blocking the files that make pages work

Google doesn't just read your text. It renders the page much like a browser does, with your styles and scripts, to see what a visitor sees.

Older robots.txt files, often written by a plugin or a past developer, sometimes block the folders that hold those style and script files. Google then sees a broken, unstyled version of your site and may judge it on that.

If your robots.txt has lines blocking folders you don't recognize, don't delete them blindly. Find out what's in those folders first.

Mistake 4: Blocking pages you actually want found

Rules match the start of a URL, and paths are case-sensitive. That makes it easy to block more than you meant to.

A rule like Disallow: /service doesn't just block one page called "service". It also blocks /services/, /service-areas/ and every page inside them. For a Stoney Creek HVAC company, that could quietly hide every furnace and AC page it has.

When a rule looks broad, check it against your real page addresses before you trust it.

Mistake 5: Blocking AI crawlers without meaning to

Search engines aren't the only visitors now. AI companies run their own crawlers, and they read robots.txt too.

Some site owners copy a "block all AI bots" list from the internet without knowing what each line does. OpenAI, for example, documents separate crawlers for training its models and for ChatGPT's search feature, so blocking one isn't the same as blocking the other.

Whether to allow AI crawlers is your call. Just make it on purpose. If you want ChatGPT to be able to recommend your Ancaster business, check that you're not turning its crawler away. The separate llms.txt file is a different idea entirely, explained in what llms.txt is.

Mistake 6: Treating robots.txt as security

Robots.txt is public. Anyone can open it in a browser.

Listing a private folder in it doesn't protect that folder. It advertises it. Anything genuinely private needs a password, not a polite request to well-behaved crawlers.

How to check yours in a couple of minutes

  1. Open the file. Type your domain followed by /robots.txt. Look for Disallow: / under User-agent: *.
  2. Open Search Console's robots.txt report. It's under Settings. It shows the version Google last fetched and flags any errors.
  3. Inspect your most important page. Paste its URL into the URL Inspection tool. If crawling is blocked, it says so.
  4. Check the Pages report. Look for pages listed as "Blocked by robots.txt" and ask whether each one should be.

If the file is wrong, fix it, then request indexing for your key pages in URL Inspection. If your pages are allowed but still not showing, the next thing to read is "Discovered – currently not indexed" and how to fix it.

What a sensible robots.txt looks like for a local business

For most small local websites, the right robots.txt is short. Let every crawler in, keep out only the pages that genuinely shouldn't be crawled, and point to your sitemap.

If you're on Wix, Squarespace or Shopify, the platform writes the file for you and its defaults are usually sensible. The risk there is a setting that hides the whole site, not the file itself. On WordPress or a custom site, you or your developer control it directly, so read it after every major change.

Frequently asked questions

Does my website need a robots.txt file?

No. Without one, search engines simply crawl what they can find. Many site builders create one for you anyway. What matters is that, if you have one, it isn't blocking pages you want in Google.

Will fixing robots.txt bring my rankings back straight away?

Not instantly. Google needs to re-read the file and then recrawl your pages. Requesting indexing for your most important pages in Search Console helps it along. How long it takes varies from site to site.

Does robots.txt affect my Google Business Profile?

Your Business Profile can still appear on Maps. But Google says a business's position in web results also plays a part in local ranking, so a site Google can't read is a handicap in the map pack too.

Is robots.txt the same as a noindex tag?

No. Robots.txt controls whether a page is crawled. A noindex tag controls whether a crawled page is shown in results. To keep a page out of Google, use noindex and leave the page crawlable.
