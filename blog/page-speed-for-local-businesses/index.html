Page speed SEO for local businesses: what actually matters

Speed won't rank a weak page, but a slow page loses the customer Google already sent you. Here's how to tell if yours is too slow, and what to fix first.

Kirill Anikin · October 30, 2026 · 5 min read

Key takeaways

  • Page speed is part of how Google judges page experience, but relevance comes first. Speed matters most because slow pages lose customers.
  • We've measured local sites whose main content took 27.7 seconds and 9.9 seconds to appear on a phone.
  • Test your key pages free in PageSpeed Insights, on the mobile tab, not just your homepage.
  • The usual culprits are oversized photos, sliders, background video and too many add-ons. Most are cheap to fix.

27.7 seconds. That's how long one site we audited took to show its main content on a phone.

Count it out loud. Now picture a homeowner in Stoney Creek with no heat on a January night, staring at a blank screen for that long. They don't wait. They tap back and call the next company.

That's the real cost of a slow site. Not a lower score in some tool, but a phone call that goes to someone else.

Does page speed affect SEO?

Yes, but probably less than you've been told.

Google uses a set of measures called Core Web Vitals as part of how it judges page experience. A faster, steadier page can have a small advantage. But Google is clear that it still shows the most relevant page first, even when that page's experience isn't great.

So speed is a tie-breaker, not a trump card. A fast page about the wrong thing won't outrank a slower page that answers the search.

Here's the part that matters more. Even when a slow page ranks, it bleeds visitors. You did the hard work of getting found, and then lost the customer at the door.

The three things Google measures, in plain English

Core Web Vitals have intimidating names. What they measure is simple.

  • Largest Contentful Paint (LCP): how long until the main thing on the page, usually the big photo or headline, actually appears. This is the one behind our 27.7-second and 9.9-second findings.
  • Interaction to Next Paint (INP): how quickly the page responds when someone taps a button or opens a menu.
  • Cumulative Layout Shift (CLS): whether things jump around while the page loads. You go to tap "Call now", a banner loads above it, and you tap "Get directions" instead.

Google explains each one in its guide to Core Web Vitals and search results. You don't need to memorize thresholds. PageSpeed Insights colours each result green, orange or red for you.

How to test your own site for free

Go to PageSpeed Insights, paste in a page address and run it. Then look at the mobile results, because a customer with a burst pipe or a dead furnace is often searching on a phone.

You'll see two kinds of data:

  • Field data comes from real Chrome users who visited your site. It's the most honest picture, but small sites often don't have enough visitors for Google to show it.
  • Lab data is a simulated test run on the spot. It's always available, and it's what you'll use to find and fix problems.

Test the pages that bring in work, not just the homepage. If your best page is "emergency plumber Hamilton Mountain", that's the one a panicking customer lands on. Test it.

What actually slows local business websites down

In our audits, the cause is almost never mysterious. It's usually one of these.

Photos straight off the camera

A photo from a modern phone is far bigger than any screen needs. Upload a gallery of them as they are, and your page is carrying a huge weight before anyone sees a word.

A Dundas renovation company wants to show off its kitchens, and it should. It just needs to resize and compress those photos first.

Sliders and background video

A rotating banner of photos, or a video playing behind the headline, looks impressive in a design meeting. On a phone in a parking lot at Limeridge Mall, it's mostly a loading bar.

Too many add-ons

Chat widgets, review widgets, pop-ups, booking tools, social feeds, tracking scripts. Each one seems small. Together, they add up to a lot of extra code a phone has to load before the page is usable.

Heavy themes and page builders

Some templates load a lot of code for features you'll never use. The site looks simple, but the phone is downloading everything the template could possibly do.

Fixes, in order of value

  1. Resize and compress your images. Usually the biggest win for the least money. Modern formats like WebP help further.
  2. Load only what's on screen first. Images lower down the page can wait until someone scrolls. The main photo at the top shouldn't wait.
  3. Remove what you don't use. That old chat widget nobody answers? The plugin from a promotion that ended long ago? Delete them.
  4. Replace the slider with one strong photo. One good image of your crew at work does more than a stack that rotates.
  5. Look at hosting and platform last. Sometimes the foundation is the problem, but check the cheap fixes first.

What you can control depends on your platform

On Wix, Squarespace and Shopify, the platform handles hosting and much of the code. You can't tune the engine, but you control images, apps and how much you put on each page. That's often enough.

On WordPress, you control nearly everything, which cuts both ways. Plugins, theme and hosting all affect speed. Our page on WordPress SEO covers the usual trouble spots.

Fast static sites, which we also build, are quick by design because there's very little for the phone to download. They suit businesses that want a simple site that loads instantly.

When speed isn't your problem

If your site doesn't show up at all, speed is unlikely to be the reason. A blocked site, unindexed pages or missing service pages will hold you back far more.

That's why speed sits in the middle of our technical SEO audit playbook, not at the top. Check that Google can get in and read your pages first. Then make them fast. If you're not sure which problem you have, start with why your website isn't ranking.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a perfect PageSpeed score?

No. The score is a lab test, and chasing perfection often costs more than it's worth. Aim for pages that load quickly and stay steady for real visitors on a phone.

Why does my score change every time I test?

Lab tests vary a little from run to run, because network conditions and server response change. Look at the pattern across a few runs, and at field data when you have it.

Will a faster website rank higher?

Possibly a little, if your competitors' pages are equally relevant. Speed won't lift a page that doesn't match the search. Its bigger payoff is keeping the visitors you already get.

Is my website builder making my site slow?

Sometimes, but more often it's what's been added on top: big photos, sliders and apps. Fix those first. If the site is still slow, then it's worth looking at the platform.
