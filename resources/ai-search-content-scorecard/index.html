AI search content checklist: a scorecard for any page on your site

Score one page at a time on whether AI tools can reach it, quote it and trust it. Fix every zero first.

Kirill Anikin · October 30, 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • AI tools don't read your whole site. They run searches, open a few pages and build an answer from whatever is clearest.
  • Pages that get quoted answer the question early, in plain sentences that still make sense on their own.
  • Consistency is trust: your name, phone and service area should match everywhere, and your site shouldn't contradict itself.
  • Most of this is ordinary good SEO. The difference is that vague pages now lose twice: in Google and in AI answers.
  • Score a page, fix the zeros, then check what AI tools actually say about you.

Ask ChatGPT "who does furnace repair in Stoney Creek?" and it doesn't read every heating company's website. It runs searches, opens a few pages and builds an answer from whatever is clearest.

We know this because we built a tool that shows the hidden searches ChatGPT runs while it answers a question. The lesson from watching it work is simple. AI tools reward pages that are easy to find, easy to quote and easy to trust.

This AI search content checklist turns that into a scorecard you can use on any page of your site.

How to use the scorecard

  1. Pick one page. Start with your homepage or your most important service page, like "emergency plumber Hamilton Mountain".
  2. Score each row 0, 1 or 2. The tables describe what a 2 and a 0 look like. Anything in between is a 1.
  3. Add it up, but don't obsess over the total. The zeros are what matter. Each one is a reason an AI tool might skip you.
  4. Fix the zeros, then the ones. Then re-score and write the date next to the result.

Part 1: Can AI tools reach the page?

A page an AI tool can't reach can't be quoted. This part is about the plumbing, not the writing.

CheckScores 2 whenScores 0 whenScore
The page is indexed by GoogleURL Inspection in Search Console says it's on GoogleIt isn't indexed, or you've never checked
robots.txt allows the crawlers you wantYou've read it and made a deliberate choice about search and AI crawlersYou don't know what it says
The key facts are written as textServices, areas and answers are in the page textThey only appear inside images, PDFs or pop-ups
Your site has an accurate llms.txt fileIt exists and matches your current servicesIt exists but is out of date. No file at all scores 1, since it's optional

A note on llms.txt: it's a proposed plain-text file that summarizes your site for AI tools. Not every AI tool reads it, so it's a bonus, not a foundation. Our explainer on what llms.txt is covers when it's worth the effort.

Part 2: Is the answer easy to find and quote?

This is where local pages often lose. They open with "Welcome to our website!" and bury the answer three scrolls down.

CheckScores 2 whenScores 0 whenScore
The main question is answered near the topThe first few lines say what you do, where and for whomThe answer is buried under a welcome speech
Headings are clear questions or topics"How much does furnace repair cost in Stoney Creek?""Welcome", "Our story", "Learn more"
Paragraphs make sense on their ownAny paragraph could be quoted alone and still be rightParagraphs lean on "as mentioned above"
Steps and comparisons use lists or tablesThe process is a numbered list, and options sit in a tableEverything is one long block of text
The page covers one serviceOne page, one intent: "drain cleaning in Hamilton"Furnaces, AC, water heaters and duct cleaning all on one page

The "on their own" row matters more than it looks. An AI answer often lifts a single sentence or two. "We serve the Mountain, the lower city, Stoney Creek and Ancaster" can be quoted. "We serve all the areas listed above" can't.

Part 3: Are the facts specific and consistent?

When your website, your Business Profile and the directories agree, you look like a real business. When they don't, anything trying to work out the facts about you has to guess.

CheckScores 2 whenScores 0 whenScore
Name, phone and area match your Business ProfileCharacter for characterA different name, an old number, or different areas
The page gives specificsWhat's included, which areas, your hours, how pricing works"Quality service at great prices"
The site never contradicts itselfOne review count, one set of hours, one list of areasDifferent pages say different things
Every number can be backed upYou could show the sourceRound-number boasts nobody can check
No leftover template textOnly your cities, your number, your projectsAnother city or phone number appears anywhere
The page shows when it was last updatedA recent, real dateNo date, or one from years ago

Every zero in this part is something we've seen on live sites: three different review counts on one website, unsupported numbers on a homepage, a paragraph naming a different Ontario city, and a fake phone number from a cloned template on 19 pages. To a person, those are typos. To a machine trying to decide what's true, they're contradictions.

Part 4: Does it show real experience?

Google says it values content that shows first-hand experience, and a page with real detail simply has more worth quoting. You can't fake experience, but you can make sure it shows.

CheckScores 2 whenScores 0 whenScore
A real person is namedThe owner or author is named, with what they doNobody is named anywhere
Photos show your own workReal jobs, real team, real vehiclesOnly stock photos
The page has first-hand detailThings only someone who did the job would knowIt could describe any company in any city
Reviews are easy to findThe page links to your Business Profile reviewsNo sign of what customers think

Here's a quick test for first-hand detail. Swap your city name for "Calgary". If the page still reads fine, it's generic. A landscaper in Waterdown who mentions the newer subdivisions and the older core around Smokey Hollow is clearly local. A page that only says "we serve your area" isn't.

Part 6: Does the rest of the web agree?

AI answers don't only come from your website. They also draw on what other sites say about you.

CheckScores 2 whenScores 0 whenScore
Your Business Profile is completeRight categories, real services, current hours, recent photosHalf-empty or out of date
Directory listings matchSame details on the main maps and directoriesOld addresses or numbers still out there
Other sites mention youLocal news, associations or suppliers mention you by nameNobody outside your own site does

Check what the AI tools actually say

A scorecard tells you what to fix. It doesn't tell you whether it worked. For that, ask the tools directly.

  • Ask ChatGPT and Gemini the questions your customers ask: "Who's a good emergency plumber on Hamilton Mountain?" "Which accountants in Ancaster do small business taxes?" Then search the same questions on Google and see whether an AI Overview appears.
  • Write down who gets named, and which websites get cited.
  • Ask the same questions again after your fixes have had time to be picked up.

Answers vary from one session to the next, so look for patterns rather than one result. Our guide on checking whether AI recommends your business walks through a fair way to test.

FAQ

Is writing for AI search different from writing for Google?

Mostly, no. Clear answers, one topic per page, real details and consistent facts help in both. The names change (SEO, AEO, GEO), but the work overlaps heavily. Our plain-English guide to AEO vs GEO vs SEO explains what actually differs.

Which pages should I score first?

Your homepage and the service pages that bring in the most valuable work. If you're an HVAC company, that's probably furnace repair before duct cleaning. Score those, fix them, then move on.

Can anyone guarantee ChatGPT will recommend my business?

No. Nobody controls what an AI tool says, just as nobody can guarantee a Google ranking. What you can do is make your business the easiest one to find, quote and trust.

How often should I re-score a page?

After any real change to the page, and every few months otherwise. Keep the old scorecards with their dates, so you can see what changed.
