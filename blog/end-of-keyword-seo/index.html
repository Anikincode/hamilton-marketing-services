Is keyword SEO dead? What changed, and what still works

Every year someone announces the death of keywords. The truth is narrower: one way of using them died long ago, and the useful part matters as much as ever.

Kirill Anikin · October 30, 2026 · 5 min read

Key takeaways

  • Keyword stuffing is dead, and Google's spam policies say so. Keywords themselves are not.
  • Customers still type words. "Emergency plumber Hamilton Mountain" is a keyword, and the business whose page matches it gets the call.
  • Google now understands meaning, so plan pages around intent, not every possible wording.
  • Even AI assistants run searches behind the scenes to answer questions. Your page still has to be the best result for those searches.

"Keywords are dead." You'll hear it from people selling AI tools, and from people who never got keywords to work.

Here's the problem with that claim. Picture someone on the Mountain with a flooded basement. They pick up their phone and type "emergency plumber Hamilton Mountain". That's a keyword. The plumber whose page matches it gets the job.

So no, keyword SEO isn't dead. But the way most people learned it is. Let's separate the two.

What actually died

A lot of old SEO advice treated keywords like a password. Type the exact phrase enough times and the door would open.

That approach is gone, for good reasons:

  • Keyword stuffing. Repeating "Hamilton plumber" over and over down a page. Google's spam policies name keyword stuffing directly. It can hurt you.
  • One page per wording. Separate pages for "plumber Hamilton", "Hamilton plumber" and "plumbers in Hamilton". They're the same search to Google, so those pages just compete with each other.
  • Writing for robots. Awkward sentences like "Our Hamilton plumber Hamilton services serve Hamilton." Nobody reads that and calls.

Google got much better at understanding meaning. It knows "AC repair" and "air conditioner repair" are the same job. It knows someone searching "furnace making a banging noise" probably needs a repair. You don't need to cram in every version.

What didn't die

People still search with words, and the words they choose still matter.

Look at our own industry in Hamilton. "seo hamilton" averages 563 searches a month. "local seo hamilton", which sounds like nearly the same thing, averages 20. A page titled with the first phrasing is speaking the customer's language. A page titled with the second is speaking ours.

We saw the same pattern when we studied a small agency's website. Its one town page titled with the exact search ("Local SEO Company in [Town]") ranked, and brought in half its traffic. Its other town pages had generic titles. They didn't rank.

Google understands meaning. But when your title, heading and opening use the words people actually type, you remove all doubt about what your page is for.

Keywords are now about intent

Think of a keyword as a label on a need, not a phrase to repeat.

"Furnace repair Stoney Creek", "furnace not working Stoney Creek" and "furnace repair near me" (typed in Stoney Creek) carry the same need: my heat is broken, fix it. One page serves all of them.

"Furnace installation Stoney Creek" carries a different need. That person is replacing a furnace, comparing options, thinking about cost. It needs its own page.

That's the modern use of keywords. You research them to understand what people need, then build one page for each need. Our guide to local keyword research shows how to do it step by step.

Even AI search runs on searches

This is the part the "keywords are dead" crowd misses.

When you ask ChatGPT who to call for a furnace repair, it doesn't always answer from memory. It can run web searches behind the scenes, read the results, and build its answer from them. We built a tool that shows those hidden searches, because they tell us what a business needs to rank for to be found.

Google's AI Overviews work from pages in Google's index too, with links to the sources. So when someone asks an AI tool a question, the old question still applies: is your page the best result for the searches it runs?

We cover how this works in more depth in generative engine optimization.

Tracking keywords still matters. Track the right ones.

Keyword tracking is one of our three pillars, but not the old way. Tracking hundreds of vanity terms from one office computer tells you very little.

What we track are the searches that lead to calls, measured across your service area. An Ancaster HVAC company doesn't care where it ranks for "HVAC" in general. It cares whether it shows up for "furnace repair Ancaster" from homes in Ancaster, Dundas and on the Mountain.

Local results change with the searcher's location, so we check rankings from a grid of points across the area. That's explained in geo-grid rank tracking.

A quick before and after

Here's how the two approaches look on the same kind of page: furnace repair for a heating company in Stoney Creek.

The old way: a title like "Stoney Creek Furnace Repair | Furnace Repair Stoney Creek | Furnace Repair Hamilton". An opening paragraph that repeats the phrase in every sentence. Separate, near-identical pages for "furnace repairs", "furnace fix" and "furnace service".

The way that works now: one page. The title says "Furnace Repair in Stoney Creek | Your Company". The H1 says the same thing in plain words. The opening tells a cold homeowner what you fix, where you work and how to book. The rest answers what they actually ask: how soon can you come, what does a repair usually cost, and when is it time to replace instead.

The keyword is still there, in the places that count. It's just not trying to do all the work anymore.

How to use keywords without sounding like a robot

  1. Pick one main search per page. The one that best describes the need the page serves.
  2. Use it where it counts. In the title, the H1, the URL and the first couple of sentences. Once each is plenty.
  3. Then write for the customer. Answer their questions in plain words. Related terms will appear naturally because you're covering the topic properly.
  4. Add what only you know. Real prices, real jobs, real answers. That's what makes the page worth ranking, as we explain in information gain.
  5. Check Search Console. It shows the actual searches that found you. Some will surprise you, and some deserve their own page.

Frequently asked questions

Do I still need keywords in my title tag?

Yes. The title is one of Google's clearest clues about a page, and it's what searchers read before they click. Put the service and area in it, in the words customers use.

Should I stop doing keyword research?

No. Keyword research is how you learn what customers need and how they describe it. What's changed is how you use it: one page per need, not one page per wording.

Does AI search use keywords?

Behind the scenes, yes. Tools like ChatGPT can run web searches to answer questions, and Google's AI Overviews draw on pages in its index. Ranking for the right searches still matters.

Should I write "near me" on my pages?

You don't need to. Google works out "near" from the searcher's location and where your business works. What helps is being clear on your pages and Business Profile about the areas you serve.
