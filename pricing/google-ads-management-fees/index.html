Pricing guide

Google Ads management fees: what you're actually paying for

Every Google Ads bill has two parts, and people mix them up. One goes to Google. The other pays whoever runs the account. Here's how both work.

Kirill Anikin · October 30, 2026 · 5 min read

Key takeaways

  • Ad spend goes to Google. The management fee goes to whoever runs your ads. Keep the two separate on paper.
  • Management is usually charged as a percentage of ad spend, a flat monthly fee, a setup fee plus monthly, or per lead.
  • Good management starts with conversion tracking. Without it, nobody can tell which clicks became calls.
  • The ad account should be in your name, billed to your card, with access you control.

You get a bill for Google Ads. How much of it went to Google, and how much went to the people running the ads?

If you can't answer that, it's the first thing to fix. Here's how ad spend and management fees work, and how to tell whether you're getting your money's worth.

Ad spend vs management fee

Ad spend is what you pay Google. With search ads, you pay when someone clicks your ad, and you set the budget.

The management fee is what you pay a person or company to set up, run and improve the campaigns. It covers their time and skill, not the clicks.

The cleanest setup: the ad account is in your name, Google bills your card directly and the manager sends a separate invoice for their fee. That way you always see exactly what Google got.

How management fees are charged

Percentage of ad spend

The fee is a share of what you spend on ads. Spend more, and the fee rises with it.

The upside: the fee grows as the account grows and takes more work. The catch: the manager earns more when you spend more, even if the extra spend isn't bringing in more calls.

Flat monthly fee

A fixed fee for an agreed scope, whatever you spend on ads.

The upside: it's predictable, and nobody has a reason to push your budget up. The catch: if your account grows a lot, the fee may need to change, so agree in advance when that happens.

Setup fee plus monthly

A one-time fee to build the account properly, then an ongoing fee to run it. Setup covers the heavy lifting: research, campaign structure, ad copy and tracking.

The upside: the build gets the attention it needs. The catch: check what the setup fee actually buys, and whether you keep all of it if you leave.

Pay per lead

Some companies charge per lead instead. It sounds safe. But read the definition of a "lead" closely. A caller who hangs up straight away, or a spam form, might count.

What management should include

  • Conversion tracking, first. Calls and form leads should be counted before a cent is spent. We've seen Google Ads accounts running with no conversion tracking at all, so nobody could tell which clicks became calls.
  • Searches that match buying intent. "Emergency plumber Hamilton" is a buyer. "Plumber apprenticeship Hamilton" is not. Negative keywords stop you paying for the second one.
  • Location targeting that matches your area. If you serve Hamilton, Stoney Creek and Ancaster but not Burlington, the ads shouldn't show in Burlington.
  • A sensible ad schedule. If nobody answers the phone at night, don't pay for night-time clicks, unless you're set up for emergency calls.
  • Landing pages that match the ad. An ad for furnace repair should land on a furnace repair page, not your homepage.
  • A plain monthly report. Spend, calls, leads and cost per lead.

Local Services Ads

If you're in the trades, there's another option. Local Services Ads are a separate Google ad type where you pay per lead rather than per click. In Canada they cover the trades only, so a plumber or HVAC company may qualify while a restaurant won't.

They need their own setup and attention, so ask whether a manager's fee covers them or only regular search ads. Our industry pages, like SEO for plumbers in Hamilton, cover how they fit each trade.

Red flags

  • The ad account is in the agency's name, not yours.
  • You can't log in and see your own data.
  • Ad spend and the management fee are bundled into one number, so you can't see what Google got.
  • No conversion tracking, or nobody can tell you your cost per lead.
  • A long lock-in contract with a fee to leave.

A worked example: what does a lead cost you?

Here's the maths with placeholders. Swap in your own numbers.

  • Monthly ad spend: $A
  • Monthly management fee: $M
  • Real leads in the month, calls and forms that were genuine enquiries: L
  • Cost per lead = (A + M) ÷ L

Now the test. Say a furnace repair job in Stoney Creek is worth $X in profit, and you win one job for every N leads. Each lead is worth X ÷ N to you. If your cost per lead is lower than that, the ads pay. If it's higher, something needs fixing: the targeting, the ads, the landing page or the fee.

Notice the management fee sits inside the maths. A cheap manager who wastes spend can cost you more than a pricier one who doesn't.

Common questions about Google Ads fees

Is the management fee part of my ad budget?

It shouldn't be. Your ad budget is what Google charges for clicks. The management fee is separate. If a quote gives you one combined number, ask for the split.

How much should I spend on Google Ads?

It depends on how many people search for your services, what a click costs in your trade and what a job is worth to you. Start with your core searches only, measure cost per lead, then adjust.

Can I run Google Ads myself?

Yes. The two things to get right are conversion tracking and location targeting. Without them, you'll spend money without knowing what's working.

Should I run Google Ads and SEO together?

For many local businesses, yes. Ads bring calls while SEO builds, and the search data from ads shows which terms are worth ranking for. See SEO vs Google Ads.

Do my ads need their own landing pages?

They work best when each ad lands on a page about that exact service. Someone who clicks an ad for "water heater repair Hamilton" should land on a water heater page with your phone number at the top, not on your homepage. A good service page does double duty: it helps your ads and your SEO.
