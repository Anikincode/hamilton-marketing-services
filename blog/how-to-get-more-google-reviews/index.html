How to get more Google reviews without breaking Google's rules

Happy customers rarely write reviews on their own. Here's a simple system that gets more of them to, without discounts, tricks or anything that could get your reviews removed.

Kirill Anikin · October 30, 2026 · 6 min read

Key takeaways

  • Happy customers leave reviews when you ask at the right moment and make it one tap. Without the ask, many never get around to it.
  • Ask everyone. Offering rewards for reviews, or only asking the happy ones, breaks Google's rules.
  • Reply to every review, good and bad. Your replies are read by the next customer, not just the last one.
  • Steady beats sudden. A system that runs after every job beats a one-off blitz.
  • Keep your website honest: one review source, no stale counts, no self-made review stars.

You do good work. Customers tell you so at the door. Then they go home, and nothing appears on Google.

That's normal. People don't think of reviews unless someone asks. The businesses with lots of recent reviews aren't necessarily better than you. Often they just ask, every time, in a way that's easy to say yes to.

This is how to get more Google reviews, step by step, without risking the ones you already have.

Why Google reviews matter

Google's own help pages say review count and rating play a part in local ranking, as one of the signals of how well known a business is.

But the bigger effect is on the click. Star ratings and review counts sit right next to your name in the map pack. Someone in Stoney Creek comparing furnace companies late on a cold night will read the reviews before they call anyone.

And when someone asks an AI assistant who to call, reviews are one of the places those answers draw from. A business with recent, specific reviews simply has more for anyone to go on.

First, the rules

Get these wrong and Google can remove your reviews. Break them badly and your profile itself can be restricted. So the rules come before the tactics.

  • No incentives. No discounts, gift cards, contest entries or "free service for a review". Google's rules ban them.
  • No filtering. Don't ask only the customers you think are happy, and don't send unhappy ones somewhere else first. Google's rules ban selectively asking for positive reviews.
  • No fake reviews. Not from you, your staff, your family or a service that sells them.
  • No review swaps with other businesses.
  • Don't write it for them. You can ask. You can't put words in their mouth.

Everything below stays inside those lines.

Step 1: Ask at the moment of relief

Timing is everything. The best moment to ask is when the customer feels the result, not when the invoice arrives two weeks later.

  • A furnace tech on the Mountain gets the heat back on in January. The house is warming up. That's the moment.
  • A moving crew unloads the last box in a new Waterdown subdivision. The customer is relieved it's over. That's the moment.
  • An auto shop on Upper James hands the keys back and the car drives fine. That's the moment.

The ask can be one sentence, said in person: "If you're happy with how it went, a Google review would really help us. I'll text you the link."

Step 2: Make it one tap

Every extra step loses people. "Search for us on Google, find the listing, scroll to reviews…" is too many steps.

Your Business Profile has an option to ask for reviews. It gives you a short link that opens the review box directly. Save it everywhere:

  • In a text message template on the phones your team uses
  • As a QR code on a small card left behind after the job, or on the counter
  • In your email signature and on your invoices

Step 3: Send a short follow-up the same day

The in-person ask plants the idea. The text or email makes it easy. Keep it short and personal:

Hi [first name], thanks for having us out today. If you have a minute, a Google review helps other people in Hamilton find us: [your review link]. Thanks, [your name]

Notice what it doesn't say: no reward, no "five stars please", no suggested wording. Just thanks and a link.

If they don't respond, one gentle reminder a few days later is fine. More than that starts to feel like pressure.

Step 4: Make it a habit, not a campaign

A sudden pile of reviews in one week, followed by silence, can look unnatural, and Google runs automated checks for fake reviews. A steady flow after every job looks like what it is: a busy business with happy customers.

So build it into the job, not the marketing plan:

  1. The tech or crew lead asks in person at the end of every job.
  2. The office sends the link the same day.
  3. Someone checks once a week that requests actually went out.

For a landscaping crew in Ancaster, that might mean the foreman asks when the last stone goes down, and the office texts the link that evening.

Step 5: Reply to every review

Google encourages owners to respond to reviews, and your replies are public. The person reading them is your next customer.

Replying to good reviews

Keep it short and human. Thank them by first name, mention the job naturally, and skip the copy-paste reply. "Thanks, Sarah. Glad the new water heater is keeping up with the whole family" reads like a real person. "Thank you for your feedback!" over and over doesn't.

Replying to bad reviews

Breathe first. Then:

  • Stay calm and polite, even if the review feels unfair.
  • Don't share private details about the job or the customer.
  • Acknowledge the problem, say what you'll do, and offer to sort it out by phone.
  • If you fix it, the customer may update their review. Don't ask them to delete it.

A calm, fair reply to a one-star review can do a lot for your reputation. It shows the next customer how you behave when something goes wrong.

Reviews that break the rules

You can't delete a review just because you don't like it. If a review breaks Google's policies, like spam, a review from a competitor, or abuse, you can report it from your profile. Honest criticism stays up. Your reply is your answer to it.

Don't undo it on your website

Two website habits quietly undercut good reviews.

Stale counts. An old review badge in the header, a different count on the homepage, another on the contact page. We audited one website showing three different review counts. Pick one live source, like a link to your Business Profile, or don't state a number at all.

Self-made review stars. Some sites copy their Google reviews into structured data hoping for stars in search results. Google doesn't show review stars for a business marking up reviews of itself, so it doesn't work, and it goes against Google's guidelines.

Track it monthly

Once a month, note three things: how many new reviews arrived, your average rating, and how many reviews you replied to. If the count drops, the asking stopped somewhere. That's a process problem, not a customer problem.

Reviews are one piece of a healthy profile. Our local SEO checklist covers the rest, and Google Business Profile posts give you a way to keep the listing active between reviews.

FAQ

Can I offer a discount in exchange for a Google review?

No. Google's rules ban incentives for reviews, including discounts, gifts and contest entries. Reviews gathered that way can be removed. Ask without a reward.

Can I ask only my happiest customers?

No. Selectively asking for positive reviews is against Google's rules. Ask every customer. If the work is good, the reviews will reflect it.

How many Google reviews do I need?

There's no magic number. Look at the businesses in the map pack for your main search, like "plumber Stoney Creek", and see where you stand. Then focus on a steady flow of recent reviews rather than a target.

Can I get a bad review removed?

Only if it breaks Google's policies, such as spam, a fake review or abuse. Report those from your profile. For honest criticism, a calm, helpful reply is your best tool.

Do reviews affect whether AI tools recommend me?

Reviews are one of the sources AI assistants can draw on when they describe a local business. Our guide on getting your business mentioned in ChatGPT covers the other factors.
