Service page SEO: how to write pages that rank and get calls

Your homepage introduces you. Your service pages do the selling. Here's the formula we write them to, and the mistakes that keep good businesses off page one.

Kirill Anikin · October 30, 2026 · 6 min read

Key takeaways

  • Give every service its own page. One page trying to sell five services often ranks for none of them.
  • Get the basics right first: the service and area in the title, one clear H1, and nothing left over from a template.
  • Write to a set formula: a hook, a direct answer, a preview, question-led sections, one piece of real proof, and one call to action.
  • Proof beats adjectives. A real photo or a real price range does more than "trusted and reliable".

Here's a test. Open your website on your phone and pretend your furnace just died in Ancaster on a Sunday night. Can you find, in a few seconds, a page that says "furnace repair in Ancaster" and a number to tap?

If not, that's the problem. Not your design, not your logo. The page that would turn that search into a call doesn't exist yet.

Service pages are where local SEO turns into revenue. Here's how we build them.

One service, one page

Google ranks pages, and each page has the best chance when it's about one thing. A "Services" page listing drain cleaning, water heaters, sump pumps and bathroom plumbing is trying to answer four different searches at once.

Start from your Google Business Profile. The categories and services listed there are the jobs Google already associates with you. Each one that matters to your business deserves its own page.

A Hamilton HVAC company might end up with separate pages for furnace repair, furnace installation, AC repair, AC installation and duct cleaning. Each answers a different customer with a different problem.

If two of your pages end up chasing the same search, they'll compete with each other. Our guide to keyword cannibalization explains how to avoid that.

Get the basics right before you write a word

Three things tell Google what a service page is about before it reads a single paragraph.

  • The title tag: start with the service, then the area, then your name. "Sump Pump Installation on Hamilton Mountain | Your Company".
  • The H1: the main heading on the page, saying the same thing in plain words.
  • The URL: short and matching, like /sump-pump-installation-hamilton-mountain/.

It sounds obvious. Yet in one audit, a business had 9 of 10 service pages with no H1 at all, still unfixed after four audits. We also keep finding titles that name the wrong city. The details are in title tags for local SEO.

The formula we write every service page to

Every service page we write follows the same six-part structure. It's step 6 of our Core 30 method, and it works because it matches how a worried customer reads.

1. A hook that names what they want

The first sentence should tell the searcher they're in the right place. "Water in your basement and a sump pump that won't start? We repair and replace sump pumps across Hamilton Mountain." No welcome message, no company history.

2. A short, direct answer near the top

Two or three plain sentences that answer the main question: what you do, where, and how to book. It's also the part Google and AI assistants can most easily quote.

3. A preview of what's on the page

A line or a short list telling the reader what's below: signs you need the service, how the job works, pricing, areas covered, questions. People who can see the answer is on the page are more likely to stay and read it.

4. Sections built from real questions

Use the questions customers really ask as your headings. "How long does a sump pump replacement take?" "Do I need a backup pump?" Answer each with a specific example, not a general claim.

5. One piece of real proof

A project photo from a real job, a real price range, a licence, or a short case study. Pick at least one. More on this below.

6. One call to action

One clear next step: call, or request a quote. A page that asks people to call, email, follow you on social media and sign up for a newsletter gives them too many choices.

Where the questions come from

You don't have to guess what customers want to know. They're already telling you.

  • "People also ask" in Google. Search your service and area, and read the questions box. Click a few and more appear.
  • Your phone. Write down every question callers ask for a couple of weeks. Those are your headings.
  • Search Console. Once the page is live, the Performance report shows the questions people searched before finding you.

A Dundas renovation company might hear "Do you handle the permit?" all the time. That question belongs on the page, answered plainly.

Proof beats adjectives

"Trusted. Reliable. Professional. Affordable." So many pages say the same four words that they've stopped meaning anything.

What does mean something is proof only you can show: a photo of a finished deck in Waterdown, a real price range from your recent jobs, the licence number for your trade, a short story about a job and how you solved it.

Be careful with numbers, though. We've audited a homepage carrying made-up-looking figures, which is a real risk before you pay to send traffic there. We've also seen one website show three different review counts in different places. If a number isn't true and consistent, leave it out. Original detail like this is what we mean by information gain.

Read every page before it goes live

Template text quietly sinks good service pages. In our audits we've found a paragraph naming a different Ontario city on several service pages, a project page copied from a template for a company in another country, and a fake phone number from a cloned template on 19 pages.

A customer in Stoney Creek who reads about a city you don't serve, or dials a number that doesn't reach you, is gone. Read every page, out loud if you have to, before it's published.

Link it into the rest of the site

A service page shouldn't float on its own. Link to it from your homepage and from the main category page above it. Link from it to the pricing page for that service, if you have one, and to the area pages where you do that work.

Then add structured data, the code that tells search engines what the page offers and who offers it. Our schema starter pack has copy-ready examples for local businesses.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a service page be?

Long enough to answer the questions a customer has before they call, and no longer. There's no magic word count. A page that answers the real questions clearly beats a longer one padded with filler.

Should I put prices on my service pages?

A real price range helps, because cost is one of the first things people want to know. If pricing is detailed, give it its own page and link to it from the service page.

Do I need a separate service page for every area I serve?

Not for every combination. Start with one strong page per service. Add area pages only where you genuinely work and can say something specific about that area.

Can I use AI to write my service pages?

AI can help with a first draft, but it doesn't know your jobs, your prices or your customers' questions. The parts that make a page rank and convert have to come from you.
