Technical SEO

A technical SEO audit playbook for local business websites

A technical SEO audit isn't a thick report full of red warnings. It's a short list of questions, asked in the right order, about whether Google can find, read and trust your site.

Kirill Anikin · October 30, 2026 · 6 min read

Key takeaways

  • Run a technical SEO audit from the foundation up: access first, then indexing, then page signals, then speed, links and consistency.
  • Every step below comes with a real problem we've found on a live local-business site.
  • A finding is only useful once it's fixed. Re-auditing a site with nothing shipped just produces the same report again.
  • Search Console and PageSpeed Insights, both free, cover most of what a small site needs.

Most technical audits we're shown are a long list of "issues" sorted by colour. Pages of warnings, no order, and no idea which one is actually costing calls.

A home inspector doesn't start with the paint. They start with the foundation, because a cracked foundation makes the paint irrelevant. A technical SEO audit should work the same way.

Here's the order we use on local business websites, and what each step has turned up in our own audits.

Step 1: Can Google get in?

Before anything else, check that search engines are allowed to crawl the site.

  • Open the robots.txt file and look for a rule that blocks everything.
  • Check key pages for a "noindex" tag.
  • Make sure the site isn't behind a password or a "coming soon" page.
  • Confirm every version of the address (with and without www, http and https) ends up at one secure version.

What we've found: a live business site whose robots.txt blocked all indexing. Google was told to ignore the whole thing. Every other fix on that site was pointless until this one was done. More on this in robots.txt mistakes.

Step 2: What's actually in Google's index?

Open Search Console's Pages report. It tells you which pages are indexed, which aren't, and why.

Compare the indexed count with the pages you actually want found. Look at both directions: important pages missing, and junk pages (test pages, tag archives, old duplicates) that got in.

What we've found: a site where Google had indexed about 5 of its 48 pages. The rest were listed as "Discovered – currently not indexed". Google knew about them and hadn't bothered to read them. We cover what causes that in "Discovered – currently not indexed", explained.

Step 3: Does every page have one clear job?

Now look at each important page on its own. Does its title, its main heading and its address all say the same service and place?

  • One H1 per page that names the service.
  • A title tag that starts with the service and names the right area.
  • No two pages chasing the same search.

What we've found: a business with 9 of 10 service pages missing an H1, unfixed after four audits. A page aimed at one part of Toronto with a different neighbourhood in its title. And a new page that replaced the homepage on six searches, after which all six dropped.

Imagine a Hamilton Mountain plumbing site where the "drain cleaning" page and the homepage both target "drain cleaning Hamilton". Google has to pick, and it may pick the weaker one.

Step 4: Is there anything on the site that isn't yours?

This step isn't in most audit tools, and it's one of the most useful. Read the pages, or search the site, for text that came from somewhere else.

What we've found:

  • A paragraph naming a different Ontario city on several service pages.
  • A project page whose body was copied from a template for a company in another country.
  • A fake phone number from a cloned template on 19 pages.

A tool sees a page with a heading and words and calls it fine. A person reads it and sees a Dundas contractor's page talking about a city they've never worked in.

Step 5: Is it fast on a phone?

Run your key pages through PageSpeed Insights on the mobile tab. Focus on Largest Contentful Paint, the time until the main content appears.

What we've found: one site whose main content took 27.7 seconds to appear on mobile, and another at 9.9 seconds. Customers searching from a job site in Binbrook won't wait that long.

The usual causes and fixes are in page speed for local businesses.

Step 6: Do old addresses still work?

Pages get renamed, merged and deleted. Each one leaves an old address behind that may still have links pointing at it.

  • Check Search Console for "Not found (404)" pages that used to get traffic.
  • Make sure moved pages use a permanent redirect to their closest match.
  • Fix internal links that point to dead or redirected pages.

What we've found: pages deleted with no redirect, including one that ranked for a search with real monthly volume. That ranking was simply thrown away.

Step 7: Do the facts match everywhere?

Your business details live in many places: your site, your Business Profile, directories and your structured data. When they disagree, Google and your customers are left guessing which one is right.

  • Is the business name, address and phone number the same on the site, the Business Profile and the main directories?
  • Does your structured data (the code that describes your business to search engines) match what the page says?
  • Are the numbers on the site true and consistent?

What we've found: two separate businesses sharing one phone number across their listings, which confuses Google about which business is which. One website showing three different review counts. And a homepage carrying made-up-looking numbers, a real risk before you pay to send traffic there.

Step 8: Who's linking to you?

Most small local sites have few links, and that's fine. What you're checking for is anything unnatural.

What we've found: a network of 60 spam domains pointing links at a small business site. We built a disavow file for it, a list that tells Google to ignore those links.

Google says most sites don't need its disavow tool, and that its systems are built to ignore spammy links. We only use it when there's a clear pattern like that one.

Turning findings into a fix list

An audit that ends with a list of problems is half done. Sort every finding into one of four buckets:

  1. Hides pages from Google. Blocked crawling, noindex, unindexed key pages. Fix these first.
  2. Confuses Google about what or where. Missing H1s, wrong-city titles, competing pages, mismatched details.
  3. Loses visitors. Slow mobile pages, broken forms, a phone number you can't tap.
  4. Polish. Everything else.

Give each fix an owner and a date to recheck. Then actually ship them. We've seen the same problems survive audit after audit, and another audit never fixed a thing. Once the fixes are live, recheck in Search Console.

The tools you need

For a small local site, Google Search Console and PageSpeed Insights do most of the work, and both are free. A site crawler helps you check titles, headings and broken links across every page. Paid suites like Ahrefs, Semrush and Moz exist too, but you don't need one to run this playbook.

Frequently asked questions

How often should I run a technical SEO audit?

After any big change, such as a redesign, a platform move or a batch of new pages. Between those, a monthly look at Search Console's Pages and Performance reports catches most new problems early.

Can I do a technical SEO audit myself?

Yes, most of it. Steps 1, 2, 4 and 5 need nothing more than a browser, Search Console and PageSpeed Insights. Redirects, structured data and link checks take a bit more experience.

What's the difference between a technical audit and a full SEO audit?

A technical audit asks whether Google can find, read and trust your site. A full audit also looks at your Business Profile, your content, your competitors and where you rank. Our SEO audit covers both.

Do I need to fix every warning an audit tool shows?

No. Many tool warnings are minor or don't apply to a small site. Fix what hides pages or confuses Google first, then decide whether the rest is worth the time.
