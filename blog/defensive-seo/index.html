Defensive SEO: how to protect the rankings you already have

Most SEO is about winning new rankings. Defensive SEO is about not losing the ones you've already earned, and from what we see in audits, losing them is easier than most owners think.

Kirill Anikin · October 30, 2026 · 6 min read

Key takeaways

  • Defensive SEO means protecting the rankings, pages and listings you already have from spam links, deleted pages, broken redirects and careless edits.
  • It's one of our three pillars, because the losses we find in audits are often self-inflicted, and they rarely announce themselves.
  • Real examples from our audits: a network of 60 spam domains pointing at a small business site, pages deleted with no redirect, changes nobody logged, and three different review counts on one website.
  • The core habits are simple: log every change, redirect every removed page, watch Search Console and track rankings across your service area.

It took years to get your plumbing company onto the first page for "drain cleaning Hamilton". It can take one afternoon to lose it.

A new website goes live and the old pages vanish. Someone edits a title. A pile of spam links appears out of nowhere. Nobody notices until the phone goes quiet.

That's what defensive SEO is for.

What is defensive SEO?

Defensive SEO is the work of protecting what you've already earned in search: your rankings, your pages, your Business Profile and your reputation.

Think of it like home maintenance. You check the roof and clear the eavestroughs before the leak, not after the ceiling comes down. The work is boring and cheap. The damage it prevents is neither.

It's one of our three pillars, alongside keyword tracking and AI implementation, because in our audits a lot of lost ground turns out to be self-inflicted.

The threats we actually find

Spam link networks

We found a network of 60 spam domains pointing links at a small business's website. Nobody had asked for those links, and nobody would have noticed without looking.

Google says its systems are built to ignore most spammy links, so most site owners never need to do anything. But a pattern that large is worth a precaution. We built a disavow file: a list that asks Google to ignore links from those domains.

Google's own guidance on disavowing links is clear that this is for specific cases, not routine use. Don't disavow links just because a tool labels them "toxic".

Pages deleted with no redirect

This one is easy to do, and it hurts. A page gets deleted or renamed during a tidy-up or a redesign, and nobody sets up a redirect.

In one case we found, a deleted page had been ranking for a search with real monthly volume. Once it was gone, that ranking went with it. Anyone who clicked an old link got an error page instead.

A 301 redirect sends visitors and search engines from the old address to the new one, and tells Google the move is permanent, so the old page's standing can follow. Every removed or renamed page needs one. Our guide to website redesigns without losing rankings covers how to map them.

Changes nobody logged

Rankings drop. You ask what changed. Nobody knows, because nobody wrote anything down.

We've seen it on real sites. Fixes get shipped without a note. Titles get edited. New pages appear. When rankings move, there's no way to connect the change to the result.

New pages can do damage too. On one site, a new page started competing with the homepage for the same searches. It "replaced" the homepage on six searches, and all six dropped. One page per search intent prevents this. We explain it in one page, one keyword.

Contradictions on your own site

We've found three different review counts shown on the same website. A homepage says one number, a service page another, the footer a third.

It looks careless to customers. To search engines and AI tools trying to work out which facts are true, it's a reason to trust none of them. The same goes for a wrong city in a title, or template text naming a different Ontario city on several service pages.

A blocked site

We once found a live business website whose robots.txt file blocked all indexing. Google was being told to ignore the entire site.

One easy way this happens is a "hide from search engines" setting left switched on after a site is built or rebuilt. One line in a file can undo everything else.

Business Profile changes you didn't make

Anyone can suggest an edit to your Google Business Profile, and Google sometimes applies suggested edits. Your hours, category, phone number or address can change without you knowing.

Check your profile regularly, and look for notifications about updates from Google.

The defensive SEO routine

  1. Keep a change log. Date, page, what changed and who changed it. A shared spreadsheet is enough.
  2. Redirect before you delete. Never remove or rename a page without a 301 redirect to its closest replacement.
  3. Check Search Console monthly. Look at the Pages report for anything that dropped out of the index, and the Performance report for pages losing clicks.
  4. Track rankings across your service area. Map rankings change with the searcher's location, so check from many points, not one. That's how we catch drops early. See geo-grid rank tracking.
  5. Review your Business Profile. Categories, hours, phone, services. Undo any edits you didn't make.
  6. Check robots.txt and noindex settings after any website update.
  7. Glance at new links now and then. Act only on clear patterns, like a large network of spam sites.
  8. Keep facts consistent. One review count, one phone number, one correct city, everywhere.

What to do when rankings drop

Don't panic-edit. Rewriting your best page the day after a drop can make things worse. Work through it in order instead:

  1. Check your change log. What changed in the last few weeks?
  2. Check Search Console's Pages report. Did important pages drop out of the index?
  3. Check robots.txt and noindex settings.
  4. Check that old URLs still redirect properly.
  5. Check your Business Profile for edits you didn't make.
  6. Compare your geo-grid with last month. Is the drop everywhere or in one part of town?
  7. Look for a new page competing with an old one for the same search.

Work through the list and you'll often find the cause. The change log makes it much faster.

Defensive SEO is the least glamorous of the three. It's also the one that protects everything else. There's no point winning "furnace repair Stoney Creek" this year if a careless redesign hands it back next year.

FAQ

Do I need to disavow spammy links?

Usually not. Google says its systems ignore most spammy links, and its guidance says disavowing is for specific cases. We only build a disavow file when there's a clear, large pattern, like the 60-domain network we found.

Can a competitor hurt my rankings with bad links?

Google says its systems are designed to ignore most spam links, so most owners never need to act. If you see a large, obvious pattern of spam sites linking to you, it's worth a look and possibly a disavow file.

What is a 301 redirect?

It's a permanent forwarding address for a web page. When a page moves or is deleted, a 301 sends visitors and Google to the new page instead of an error, and helps keep the old page's rankings.

How often should I check for problems?

Monthly for Search Console, rankings and your Business Profile. Check robots.txt, noindex settings and redirects every time your website changes, not just on a schedule.
