Local keyword research for service businesses, step by step

Local keyword research isn't about finding the biggest number. It's about finding the searches that turn into calls, and giving each one a page.

Kirill Anikin · October 30, 2026 · 6 min read

Key takeaways

  • Start from your Google Business Profile categories and services, not from a keyword tool.
  • Group searches by intent. Each group becomes one page.
  • Read search volumes as rough estimates. We've seen a tool put a site's traffic about five times higher than Search Console showed.
  • Check the whole year, not one month. In our own industry, every "SEO + city" search swings about tenfold between seasons.

The biggest number in a keyword tool is rarely the search that pays.

"Plumbing" is a big, vague search. The person typing it could be a student, a weekend DIYer or someone on the other side of the country. "Drain cleaning Hamilton" is far smaller, but the person typing it almost certainly has a drain problem in Hamilton.

That's the whole idea of local keyword research. Find the searches that turn into calls, then build a page for each one. Here's how we do it, step by step.

Step 1: Start from your Business Profile, not a keyword tool

Your Google Business Profile's primary category decides which searches you can show up for on Maps. Your secondary categories and your services list widen that. So before you research a single keyword, get those right.

We've audited a profile with one category and a list of stock services the business didn't even sell. Every keyword plan built on that profile would have started from the wrong place.

List your real categories and every service you actually want to sell. That list is the skeleton of your website. Our guide to Google Business Profile categories explains how to choose them.

Step 2: Write each service the way customers say it

You call it one thing. Your customers may call it another.

An HVAC technician might say "heat pump installation" while a homeowner types "AC and heating unit". A window cleaner might say "exterior glass cleaning" while the customer searches "window cleaning" or "eavestrough cleaning". Write down both versions.

Your best source here is your own phone. For a couple of weeks, write down the exact words callers use. They're often different from the words on your website.

Step 3: Add the places, the way people search them

Local searches usually take one of three shapes:

  • Service + city: "furnace repair Hamilton".
  • Service + area: "furnace repair Stoney Creek" or "furnace repair Hamilton Mountain".
  • Service + "near me": "furnace repair near me", where Google fills in the location from the searcher's phone.

Be ready for smaller numbers than you expect at the area level. In our own industry, "SEO" searches with area names like Stoney Creek, Ancaster or Grimsby come in at 0 to 10 a month, while the city-wide phrasings get hundreds. Many people seem to search the city name even when they live in one part of it.

That doesn't make area pages useless. The map pack changes with where the searcher stands, so being clearly connected to Ancaster still matters to someone searching from Ancaster. It just means you shouldn't judge an area page on volume alone.

Step 4: Group the searches by intent

Now sort your list. Searches with the same intent, the same reason behind them, belong together. Each group becomes one page.

Grouping first stops two pages ending up on the same search, which would make them compete. That's the rule we cover in one page, one keyword.

Step 5: Read the volume numbers with care

Keyword tools are useful. We use Ubersuggest for search volume and difficulty. But every tool is modelling, not counting.

On one site we looked at, a keyword tool estimated traffic about five times higher than Search Console showed. Tools model. Search Console measures. Use tool numbers to compare one search with another, not as a forecast of visitors.

Three more things to watch:

  • Look at 12-month averages. Search demand moves with the seasons. In our own industry, every "SEO + city" search swings about tenfold: high from September to January, low from April to August. Your trade will have its own rhythm. Judge it on the full year.
  • Phrasing matters more than you'd think. In Hamilton, "seo hamilton" averages 563 searches a month. "local seo hamilton", which sounds like the same thing, averages 20. Put the phrase people actually use in your title.
  • Small numbers aren't zero. A search with low listed volume can still bring in a high-value job. If it's a job you want, it can still deserve a page.

Step 6: Mine the questions

Every service has questions customers ask before they hire. Those are your supporting content and your FAQ answers.

  • Search your main service and area in Google and read the "People also ask" box.
  • Check the questions customers ask on your Business Profile and in reviews.
  • Ask yourself what you end up explaining on almost every quote visit.

A Waterdown landscaper might find questions like "when is the best time to lay sod" or "do I need a permit for a retaining wall". Each one is a chance to show you know your trade, and each answer can link back to the service page it supports.

Step 7: Check what's already ranking

Before you build a page, search the main keyword and look at what Google already shows. Service pages? Directories? Blog posts? That tells you what kind of page Google expects for that search.

Look at how strong the competition is, too. When we looked at "seo hamilton", the top three organic results had a domain authority of only 10 to 16. Domain authority is a score SEO tools give a site, mostly based on its links, and scores that low are a sign that a well-built page could compete. We compare markets that way in SEO competition in Hamilton vs Toronto.

Remember that local results change with location. To see where you really stand across your area, use geo-grid rank tracking rather than one search from your desk.

Step 8: Turn it into a page plan

Finish with a simple spreadsheet. One row per page, with columns for the primary keyword, the close variations, the intent, and whether the page exists yet.

That list becomes your build order: money services first, then pricing pages, then supporting questions. Once pages are live, Search Console's Performance report shows the real searches that found you, and the next round of research starts there.

Frequently asked questions

Which keyword tool should I use?

We use Ubersuggest for volume and difficulty and Google Search Console for what's really happening on your site. Ahrefs, Semrush and Moz are well-known alternatives. Whichever you use, treat the numbers as estimates.

How many keywords should one page target?

One primary keyword, plus the close variations that mean the same thing. "Furnace repair Hamilton" and "Hamilton furnace repair" share a page. "Furnace installation" gets its own.

Should I target keywords that show zero searches?

Sometimes. Tools can show low or zero volume for specific local searches that people still type. If it's a service you sell in a place you work, a good page can still earn calls from it.

How often should I redo keyword research?

Do it properly once, then review it every few months using Search Console. You'll see new searches appearing, and gaps where you have impressions but no dedicated page.
