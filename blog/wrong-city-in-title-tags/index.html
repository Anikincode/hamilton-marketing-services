Title tags for local SEO, and the wrong-city mistake we keep finding

Your title tag is the first thing a searcher reads and one of the first things Google reads. If it names the wrong place, both of them move on.

Kirill Anikin · October 30, 2026 · 6 min read

Key takeaways

  • A title tag is the blue link in Google. For a local page, it should say the service and the place, in the words customers use.
  • We keep finding titles and headings that name the wrong city or neighbourhood, usually left over from a copied page or template.
  • A small agency we studied got half its traffic from the one town page titled with the exact search. Its generically titled pages didn't rank.
  • When a page ranks but gets no clicks, the title is the first thing to fix.

Imagine a Stoney Creek restaurant with a sign out front that says "Burlington's favourite brunch". Locals would be confused. Visitors would think they'd taken the wrong exit off the QEW.

That's what a wrong-city title tag does to your page. And it's one of the most common mistakes we find.

What a title tag is, and why it matters so much locally

Every page has a title tag, a line of code you never see on the page itself. Google usually uses it as the clickable blue headline in search results.

It does two jobs at once. It tells Google what the page is about, and it tells the searcher whether this result is for them.

For a local business, "for them" means two things: the right service and the right place. Someone searching "furnace repair Ancaster" scans the results for those two ideas. If your title says "Home | Your Company", it gives them neither.

Google can rewrite titles it thinks are unhelpful, using headings or other text from the page. Its guide to title links in search results explains when. Clear, accurate titles are the ones it usually leaves alone.

The wrong-city problem

Here's what we find in audits, again and again.

  • A page targeting one part of Toronto that carried a different neighbourhood in its title.
  • A paragraph naming a different Ontario city sitting on several service pages, left over from a template for another business.

Nobody does this on purpose. It creeps in three ways.

Copying a page to make a new one

You have a good Dundas page. You copy it to make an Ancaster page and change the heading. But the SEO title, set in a separate settings box, still says Dundas. Now you have two pages that both tell Google they're about Dundas, and none about Ancaster.

Templates built for someone else

Site templates and "done for you" packages often carry text written for another business in another city. If nobody reads every page before launch, that city name goes live on yours.

Renaming a page without renaming its title

Most website builders keep the page name, the heading and the SEO title as separate fields. Change one and the others stay as they were.

Why a wrong city costs you

First, Google gets mixed signals. If the heading says Ancaster and the title says Dundas, which area is the page for? When it can't tell, it may not rank the page well for either.

Second, searchers skip it. A homeowner in Ancaster sees "Dundas" and assumes you don't work there.

Third, it creates fights between your own pages. Two pages both claiming Dundas compete for the same searches, a problem called keyword cannibalization. We explain it in one page, one keyword.

Use the words people actually type

We studied a small agency's website to see which of its town pages worked. Only one was titled with the exact search, in the form "Local SEO Company in [Town]". That page ranked, and it brought in half the site's traffic.

Its other town pages had generic, clever-sounding titles. They didn't rank.

The lesson isn't to stuff keywords. It's to say plainly what the page is, using the words a customer types. "Furnace Repair in Stoney Creek" tells someone in Stoney Creek with a broken furnace exactly what they need to know. "Comfort You Can Count On" tells them nothing.

A simple formula for local title tags

For a service page, use: [Service] in [Area] | [Business name]. Put the service first, because that's what the searcher scans for. Keep it short enough that the important words aren't cut off on a phone.

Furnace repair pageServices | Your CompanyFurnace Repair in Stoney Creek | Your Company
Area page for DundasDundasPlumber in Dundas | Your Company

Restaurant menu pageMenuBrunch Menu on Locke Street, Hamilton | Your Restaurant
Moving pageMoving Made EasyMovers in Waterdown | Your Company

One page, one area. "Plumber Hamilton Burlington Grimsby Stoney Creek Ancaster" isn't a title. It's a list, and Google's spam policies treat that kind of keyword stuffing as a problem. If you genuinely serve several areas, give each one a real page of its own.

Title, heading and URL should agree

Your title is what shows in Google. Your H1 is the main heading on the page. Your URL is the page's address. For a local page, all three should point at the same service and place.

For example: title "Sump Pump Installation on Hamilton Mountain | Your Company", H1 "Sump pump installation on Hamilton Mountain", URL /sump-pump-installation-hamilton-mountain/.

Don't skip the H1. In one audit, a business had 9 of its 10 service pages with no H1 at all, still unfixed after four audits. Every one of those pages was missing its clearest signal. The details of building the rest of the page are in how to write service pages that rank.

When the title is the only problem

Sometimes a page ranks fine and still gets no clicks.

Our own parent company's site had a page at an average position of 3 to 5 in Search Console with zero clicks from 233 impressions. Plenty of people saw it. Nobody picked it. That isn't a ranking problem. It's a title problem.

That's why our monthly maintenance includes a click-through audit. We find pages ranking 4 to 10 with weak clicks, rewrite their titles and descriptions, and recheck after 30 days.

Check your own titles in one sitting

  1. Search site:yourdomain.ca in Google and read every title it shows.
  2. Look for any city or neighbourhood you don't serve, or one that doesn't match the page.
  3. Check that each service page starts its title with the service.
  4. Open each page and compare the title with the H1. Do they agree?
  5. In Search Console's Performance report, find pages with plenty of impressions and few clicks. Rewrite those titles first.

Our SEO audit checklist includes these steps alongside the rest of an on-page check.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a title tag be?

Long enough to say the service, the place and your name, and short enough that Google doesn't cut it off on a phone. Put the most important words first, so they survive if it does get trimmed.

Should every page title include my city?

Every page that targets a local search should. Your service and area pages, for example. A privacy policy or a blog post about choosing a furnace doesn't need one.

Can one page target several cities in its title?

It shouldn't. A title listing several cities reads as keyword stuffing and asks one page to do the job of several. Give each area you genuinely serve its own page.

Why did Google change my title in the search results?

Google sometimes rewrites titles it finds too long, vague or out of line with the page. Making the title accurate and matching it to the H1 usually helps Google use yours.

Titles are a small fix with a big effect, and wrong-city titles are one of the first things our technical SEO work checks. We review every title, heading and URL on your site and make sure each page tells Google exactly what it's for and where.
