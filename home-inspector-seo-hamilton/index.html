How customers search

A planned purchase on a short clock

A home inspection is never a surprise. The buyer knew they'd need one. But the moment they need it, there's an offer signed and a condition deadline ticking. That mix of planned and urgent is what makes this trade different.

The searches split by who's looking and what kind of property it is:

  • Buyers: "home inspector Hamilton", "home inspection Ancaster", "home inspection cost Hamilton".
  • New builds: "new construction inspection Waterdown", "pre-delivery inspection Binbrook".
  • Sellers: "pre-listing inspection Stoney Creek".
  • Condos: "condo inspection downtown Hamilton".

The second audience: agents

Many buyers ask their real estate agent who to use. Agents keep a short list, and they look you up before adding you to it.

So your website has two readers. The buyer wants to know what you check and what it costs. The agent wants to know you're reliable, clear and fast with the report. Write for both.

Google Business Profile

A narrow trade with a few useful categories

"Home inspector" is the primary category for almost every inspection business. The secondaries depend on extra services you really offer.

  • Home inspector. Your primary category.
  • Commercial real estate inspector. Only if you inspect commercial buildings.
  • Water testing service. If you test well water as a regular add-on.
  • Asbestos testing service. Only if you're qualified and equipped to take and send samples.

Then use the services list for every add-on: thermal imaging, sewer camera scopes, radon testing, new-build inspections. Each one is a reason for Google to match you to a more specific search. Google sometimes renames categories, so pick the closest it offers.

Reviews from people who were stressed

Your clients are mid-purchase, often spending more money than ever before. The reviews that land mention thoroughness, patience and a report they could understand.

Ask when you deliver the report and they've had a chance to read it. Reply to each one. And never offer a discount for a review; Google's rules don't allow it.

Content that books inspections

Your best marketing is answering questions

Home inspectors have an advantage most trades don't: buyers have dozens of questions, and you know the answers. Every one you answer on your site is a chance to be found.

Service pages

Pre-purchase, pre-listing, new construction, condo and any add-on testing. One page each, with what's checked and how long it takes.

A sample report page

Show a real, anonymized example on the page itself, not only as a download. It's the most convincing thing you can publish.

Question pages

"What does a home inspection include?" "Should I get one on a new build?" Short, honest answers that link back to the right service page.

This is one of the few trades where a blog really earns its keep, if each post answers a question real buyers ask. Read does a local business need a blog? for the rules we follow, and see our SEO content service.

The housing calendar

Your busy months follow listings and sales. When the market picks up, so do searches. Build pages in the slow stretch so they're ranking when it turns.

Common website problems

What trips up inspection websites

Inspectors are detail people. Their websites often aren't, usually because they were built from a template and never checked.

  • Template leftovers. We've seen a cloned template leave a fake phone number on 19 pages of a live site. Every one of those pages was sending calls nowhere.
  • The report locked in a PDF. Google can read some PDFs, but a sample report built as a normal page is easier to find, faster to open on a phone, and easier to link to.
  • No price guidance. "Call for a quote" makes a buyer on a deadline call someone else. Explain what changes the price, even if you can't post one number.
  • No face. People want to know who's walking through the house. A real photo and a short bio do a lot of work.

FAQ

Home inspector SEO questions

Should a home inspector have a blog?

Yes, if it answers real buyer questions. Inspectors know things buyers want to learn, and each good answer is a page that can rank. Skip general news posts that nobody searches for.

Should I publish my inspection prices?

A starting price or a clear explanation of what changes it helps a lot. Buyers on a deadline don't want to wait for a callback just to learn whether you're in budget.

How do I get found by agents, not just buyers?

Make it easy for an agent to vouch for you: a sample report, a clear turnaround time you really keep, and reviews that mention clear communication. Agents look you up the same way buyers do.

Can I show up across the whole city, or only near my home?

Inspectors travel to the property, so you can list every area you cover and keep your home address off the map. Distance still counts, though. Clear service pages, steady reviews and geo-grid tracking show you where you're strong, say on the Mountain, and where you need work, like Grimsby or Waterdown.

Do new-build and condo inspections need their own pages?

Yes. Someone buying in a new Waterdown subdivision is asking different questions from someone buying a downtown condo. Separate pages let each one rank for its own search.
