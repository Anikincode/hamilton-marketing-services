The SEO proposal checklist: what a good one includes

Most SEO proposals look alike on the surface: a logo, a chart going up and three packages with metal names. This checklist shows you what to look for underneath.

Kirill Anikin · October 30, 2026 · 5 min read

Key takeaways

  • A good SEO proposal is about your business. It names real problems on your website and Business Profile, not generic ones.
  • It says exactly what will be done each month, who does it, and how you'll know it worked.
  • It never guarantees rankings. Google itself says nobody can.
  • You should own everything: your domain, website, Business Profile, Search Console and any ad account.

You asked three SEO companies for a quote. Now you have three proposals, three prices and three different ideas of what "SEO" means.

How do you compare them? Not by the price first. By what's actually inside.

Here's the checklist we'd use if we were the ones hiring. Work through it with each SEO proposal on the table, and the differences get obvious fast.

Part 1: Did they actually look at your business?

The quickest test of any SEO proposal: could it have been sent to a different business without changes?

A proposal that's really about you will mention specific things it found. The kind of findings we mean look like this:

  • "9 of your 10 service pages have no main heading."
  • "Your robots.txt is blocking Google from the whole site."
  • "Your Business Profile has one category, and its services list includes work you don't do."
  • "This page shows up in Google but gets almost no clicks, so the title needs work."

Each of those is a real problem we've found in audits. You can check every one of them yourself. Compare that with "your site needs on-page optimization and link building", which could be said about any website on earth.

Also check whether they looked at the right area. A Waterdown landscaper needs a plan for Waterdown, Flamborough and the neighbouring areas they actually serve, not a generic plan for "Hamilton".

Part 2: Is the scope specific?

Read the deliverables. Could you check, at the end of the month, whether each one happened?

Specific looks like this:

  • A named list of pages to build or rewrite, such as a page for furnace repair in Stoney Creek.
  • Business Profile work: categories, services, photos, posts, review requests.
  • Technical fixes, named.
  • How rankings will be tracked, and where.
  • What the monthly report includes.

Vague looks like "ongoing optimization", "authority building", "monthly SEO tasks" or "submission to hundreds of directories". These phrases aren't always hiding bad work. But you can't hold anyone to them.

Part 3: Does it promise the right things?

Google says plainly that no one can guarantee a #1 ranking. A proposal that guarantees one is either misunderstanding SEO or hoping you do.

Watch for related promises: "page one by next month", "guaranteed map pack", "thousands of visitors". Good proposals promise work and honesty, then show you how progress will be measured.

Timelines should be realistic too. Fixing a broken Business Profile can show results within weeks. New pages and new rankings usually take months.

Part 4: How will success be measured?

The proposal should say what you'll see each month, and it should start with calls and leads, not just rankings.

Look for:

  • Calls, forms and direction requests from your Business Profile and website.
  • Map rankings tracked across your service area, not from one spot.
  • Real data from Google Search Console, not a tool's traffic estimate.

If the proposal includes Google Ads, it should set up conversion tracking before spending anything. We've seen ad accounts running with none, so nobody could tell which clicks became calls. Our article on what your SEO report should show goes into detail.

Part 5: Who owns what?

This is the one owners regret skipping.

Your domain, website, content, Google Business Profile, Search Console and Google Ads account should all be in your business's name. The SEO company should be added as a user or manager, not as the owner.

If a proposal is silent on ownership, ask. If the answer is "we keep the website if you leave", think hard before signing.

Part 6: Price and terms

The price should be clear, and so should what's included at that price and what costs extra.

Ask about the minimum term, the notice period and what happens to the work if you cancel. Some providers ask for a minimum term and some work month to month. Either can be fair, as long as it's written down and you understand it.

Red flags that should end the conversation

  • Links from "our network". We once found a network of 60 spam domains pointing links at a small business's website. Cleaning it up meant building a disavow file to tell Google to ignore them. You don't want your name attached to a network like that.
  • Offers to write or buy reviews. Google's rules ban fake and paid reviews.
  • Adding keywords to your business name on your Business Profile. Google's guidelines ban it.
  • A virtual office or fake address to appear in another area. Also banned, and listings can be suspended for it.
  • Refusing to say what they'll do. "Proprietary methods" is not a scope.

FAQ

What should an SEO proposal include?

Specific findings about your website and Business Profile, a checkable list of monthly work, how success will be measured, who owns your accounts, and a clear price with terms. If any of those is missing, ask for it in writing.

Should an SEO proposal include an audit?

It should at least show that someone looked at your site and profile. A short audit is the best evidence of that, because you can check its findings yourself.

Is a guaranteed ranking ever legitimate?

No. Google says nobody can guarantee a #1 ranking, because nobody outside Google controls the results. Look for clear work and honest reporting instead.

How many proposals should I compare?

Two or three is usually enough to see the difference between specific and generic. Our list of questions to ask an SEO company helps you compare them on the same terms.
