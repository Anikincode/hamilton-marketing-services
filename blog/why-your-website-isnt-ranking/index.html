Why is my website not ranking? The real reasons we find

Most sites that don't rank have one or two specific, fixable problems. Here's how to find yours before you spend another dollar on marketing.

Kirill Anikin · October 30, 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Check the boring things first: whether Google is allowed into your site, and whether your pages are in its index at all.
  • Most "not ranking" problems we find have a name: a blocked site, a missing page, two pages fighting, or a title that names the wrong city.
  • Ranking and getting clicks are different problems. Search Console tells you which one you have.
  • Keyword tools estimate. Search Console measures. Judge your site on the second one.

You paid for the website. It looks good. Then you search "furnace repair Hamilton" and scroll, and scroll, and you're nowhere.

Here's the good news. When we audit local websites, the reason is rarely a mystery. On September 20, 2026 we scored 35 live local-business websites on our audit rubric, and the average was 34 out of 100.

Low scores, yes. But almost every lost point was something you could name and fix. This guide walks through the causes in the order we check them. Start at the top, because the first few can hide an entire site.

1. Is Google even allowed in?

Every website can have a small file called robots.txt. It tells search engines where they may and may not go. One line in it can lock the front door.

We've audited a live business website whose robots.txt blocked all indexing. Google was being told, every single visit, to ignore the whole site. The owner had no idea, because the site looked perfectly normal in a browser.

A "noindex" tag does the same job page by page. It's a hidden instruction that says "don't show this in search results". Designers often switch it on while a site is being built, and then nobody switches it off.

How to check: type your domain followed by /robots.txt into your browser. If you see Disallow: / under User-agent: *, you've found your problem. Our guide to robots.txt mistakes that hide your site covers the other versions of this.

2. Google found your pages but hasn't indexed them

Being allowed in isn't the same as being in. Google keeps an index, a giant catalogue of pages it's willing to show. If a page isn't in the catalogue, it can't rank for anything.

On one site we audited, Google had indexed about 5 of its 48 pages. The rest sat in Search Console as "Discovered – currently not indexed". Google knew those pages existed. It just hadn't decided they were worth reading.

That usually means the pages look thin, look alike, or aren't linked from anywhere important. We explain the fixes in what "Discovered – currently not indexed" means.

How to check: open Google Search Console, go to the Pages report under Indexing, and compare the number of indexed pages with the number you think you have. No Search Console account yet? That's step zero.

3. You don't have a page for what people search

Google ranks pages, not businesses. That's the part most owners miss.

Say someone in Stoney Creek searches "water heater replacement". If your only mention of water heaters is one bullet on a "Services" page, you're asking that bullet to beat whole pages written about exactly that job. It rarely wins.

The fix is one page per service. "Drain cleaning Hamilton" and "sump pump installation Hamilton" are different jobs, different customers and different searches. Each one deserves a page built around what that customer wants to know. Here's how to write service pages that rank.

4. Two of your pages are fighting over the same search

This one surprises people. Adding a page can make you rank worse.

We watched a business publish a new page that went after the same searches as its homepage. Google swapped the new page in for the homepage on six searches. All six dropped.

When two of your pages chase the same search, Google has to guess which one you mean, and it can keep changing its mind. It's called keyword cannibalization, and the cure is a simple rule: one page, one keyword.

5. Your titles and headings say the wrong thing

Your page title is the blue link people click in Google. It's also one of the clearest clues Google gets about what the page is for.

We keep finding titles that name the wrong place. One page aimed at one part of Toronto carried a different neighbourhood in its title. On another site, a paragraph naming a different Ontario city sat on several service pages, left over from a template built for someone else.

Picture a Dundas page with "Ancaster" in the title. Google reads it as an Ancaster page, and Dundas customers scroll right past it.

Headings matter too. One business had 9 of its 10 service pages with no H1, the main heading that tells readers and Google what a page is about. It was still unfixed after four audits.

6. You rank, but nobody clicks

Sometimes the problem isn't ranking at all.

Our own parent company's site had a page sitting at an average position of 3 to 5 in Search Console, with zero clicks from 233 impressions. People saw it. Nobody chose it. That's a title problem, not a ranking problem, and no amount of new content would have fixed it.

Search Console's Performance report shows impressions, clicks and average position side by side. If impressions are healthy and clicks are close to zero, rewrite the title and description before you touch anything else.

That's exactly what our monthly maintenance does. We rewrite titles on pages ranking 4 to 10 with weak clicks, then recheck after 30 days.

7. The page is too slow on a phone

Picture someone on the Mountain, standing in a wet basement, searching on their phone. They won't wait long for your page to load.

We measured one site whose main content took 27.7 seconds to appear on mobile. Another took 9.9 seconds. Google calls this measure Largest Contentful Paint, and you can test any page for free in PageSpeed Insights.

Speed alone rarely decides who ranks. Google says the most relevant page still comes first. But a page that slow loses the visitor even when it does rank.

8. You're reading the wrong numbers

Before you decide you're "not ranking", check where that idea came from.

Keyword tools estimate traffic from models. On one site we looked at, a popular tool put the traffic about five times higher than Search Console showed. Tools model. Search Console measures what really happened.

Your own searches mislead too. Search "plumber near me" from a shop on Upper James and you'll see different map results from a customer in Waterdown. Google's results move with the searcher's location, which is why we track rankings from a grid of points across your service area, not from one spot.

9. Or the field is simply ahead of you

If everything above checks out, the answer may just be competition and time. A site that launched last spring is up against businesses with years of pages, reviews and links.

That's not a reason to give up. It's a reason to pick your battles: specific services, specific areas, and the searches bigger names don't bother with. Google itself says nobody can guarantee a #1 ranking, so be wary of anyone who promises one.

A self-check you can do this afternoon

  1. Open your robots.txt file and look for Disallow: /.
  2. Search site:yourdomain.ca in Google to see roughly which pages are indexed.
  3. In Search Console, compare indexed pages with the pages you actually have.
  4. List your services. Does each one have its own page, with the service and area in the title?
  5. Look for two pages chasing the same search.
  6. Read every title out loud. Wrong city? Leftover template text?
  7. Run your most important pages through PageSpeed Insights on mobile.
  8. In the Performance report, look for pages with plenty of impressions and almost no clicks.

Most owners find at least one problem on that list. Fix the ones near the top first.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take a new website to rank on Google?

It depends on how competitive your service is and how much the site gives Google to work with. Fixes like a corrected title can show within weeks. New pages usually take a few months. Nobody can promise a date.

Why does my site show up for me but not for my customers?

Local results change with the searcher's location. You searching from your shop isn't the same as a customer searching from across the city. Search Console shows what real searchers saw, and geo-grid tracking shows where on the map you appear.

Will submitting my site to Google make it rank?

Submitting a sitemap in Search Console helps Google find your pages. It doesn't make them rank. Ranking still depends on each page being the best answer for the search.

Is my website not ranking because of the platform it's built on?

Rarely. We find blocked pages, missing headings and wrong-city titles on every platform. Each has its quirks, but the problems above matter far more than the logo on your website builder.

Should I just start over with a new website?

Usually not. A rebuild can lose the rankings you already have if it's done carelessly. Find the actual problem first. Often it's a fix that takes an hour, not a new site.
