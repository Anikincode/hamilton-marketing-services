Keyword cannibalization: why every page needs one keyword of its own

Adding a page should never cost you rankings. When it does, the usual cause is two of your own pages trying to win the same search.

Kirill Anikin · October 30, 2026 · 5 min read

Key takeaways

  • Keyword cannibalization is when two or more of your pages target the same search, so they compete with each other instead of with your competitors.
  • We saw a new page replace a homepage on six searches. All six dropped.
  • The rule that prevents it: one page, one intent, one primary keyword.
  • To fix it, pick a winner, then merge, redirect or re-aim the other page.

You'd never send two of your own trucks to race each other for the same parking spot. But that's what plenty of local websites do with their pages.

We watched it happen to one business. It published a new page aimed at the same searches as its homepage. Google swapped the new page in for the homepage on six searches, and all six dropped.

The new page wasn't bad. It was just competing with its own team.

What keyword cannibalization means

Keyword cannibalization happens when two or more pages on your site go after the same search with the same purpose.

Google tends to show only a page or two from any one site for a search. When you give it two candidates for the same job, it has to guess. It may switch between them from week to week, and neither page builds up the steady strength that comes from being the clear answer.

Think of a Hamilton HVAC company with a "Furnace Repair" page and a blog post called "Best Furnace Repair in Hamilton". Both are trying to win "furnace repair Hamilton". Instead of one strong page, it has two half-strength ones.

Why the six-search page dropped

We can't see inside Google, but the likely story is simple. A homepage usually has history: links pointing at it and a track record with its searches. A brand-new page has none of that.

So when Google tried the new page in the homepage's place, it couldn't hold the positions the homepage had earned, and all six searches slipped.

The fix for that isn't more content. It's deciding which page owns those searches and making that obvious to Google.

The rule: one page, one intent, one primary keyword

"Intent" is just the reason behind a search. What does the person actually want?

Searches that share the same intent belong on one page, even if the wording differs. Searches with different intent need different pages, even if they share words.

SearchWhat the person wantsWhich page

Hamilton furnace repairSame thing, words swappedSame furnace repair page
furnace repair near meSame thing, from a phoneSame furnace repair page
furnace installation HamiltonI'm replacing my furnaceA separate installation page
furnace installation cost HamiltonWhat will a new one cost me?A separate pricing page

A repair customer and an installation customer have different problems, different budgets and different questions. One page can't serve both well, and trying often means it ranks for neither.

This sorting step, clustering keywords by intent before writing anything, is part of how we plan every site. Our guide to local keyword research shows how to do it.

How cannibalization creeps into local websites

Nobody plans it. It builds up over time, one reasonable-looking page at a time.

  • Blog posts written like service pages. A post called "Why You Need Emergency Plumbing in Stoney Creek" that's really a sales page competes with your emergency plumbing page.
  • A homepage that tries to rank for everything. If your homepage targets every service, it competes with every service page.
  • Copied area pages. An Ancaster page copied from a Dundas page, with the old town left in the title, means two pages claiming Dundas. More on that in title tags for local SEO.
  • Old and new versions both live. After a redesign, the old "services" page and the new "drain cleaning" page can both survive and both chase the same search.
  • A new page aimed at the homepage's main search. Exactly what happened in our six-search example.

How to spot it in Search Console

You don't need a paid tool. Search Console shows it directly.

  1. Open the Performance report.
  2. Add a query filter for an important search, such as "drain cleaning".
  3. Switch to the Pages tab.
  4. If two or more of your pages are getting impressions for that search, look closer.

The warning sign is two pages trading places over time, or a new page appearing for a search just as the old page's position drops. A quick site:yourdomain.ca drain cleaning search in Google also shows which of your pages it associates with the topic.

How to fix it

First, pick a winner. Usually that's the page with the most history, links and clicks for the search, or the one that matches the intent best.

Then deal with the other page in one of three ways:

  1. Merge and redirect. Move anything useful from the losing page into the winner, then permanently redirect the old address to it. Don't simply delete it. Pages deleted without a redirect throw away whatever they had earned.
  2. Re-aim it. Give the losing page a different job. That "Best Furnace Repair in Hamilton" blog post could become "Repair or replace? How to decide about an old furnace", a genuine question that links to the repair page.
  3. Clarify the signals. Make the titles and headings say clearly different things, and point your internal links for that search at the winner.

After the change, give Google time to recrawl, then check the same query in Search Console. You want one page standing out instead of two splitting the impressions.

Planning so it never happens

The simplest prevention is a page map: a spreadsheet with one row per page and the one primary search it owns. Before anyone writes a new page or post, check the map. If the search is already taken, improve that page instead.

Frequently asked questions

Can two of my pages rank for the same keyword?

Sometimes, and it isn't always a problem. If both pages show steadily and both get clicks, leave them. It becomes a problem when they trade places, split the clicks, or drag each other down.

Can my homepage mention the same services as my service pages?

Yes. The homepage should mention each service briefly and link to the page that owns it. The service page is where the full detail and the targeting live.

Do area pages cause keyword cannibalization?

Not if each targets a different place and says something genuinely different. "Plumber in Dundas" and "Plumber in Ancaster" are different searches. They clash when one is a copy of the other with the wrong town left in.

Should I use a canonical tag instead of merging pages?

A canonical tag tells Google which of two near-identical pages is the main one. It suits genuine duplicates. For two different pages fighting over a search, it's usually better to merge or re-aim them.
