How to get mentioned in ChatGPT when customers ask who to call

ChatGPT doesn't pick businesses out of thin air. It searches, reads and repeats what it finds. Here's how to make sure it finds you, and gets your details right.

Kirill Anikin · October 30, 2026 · 6 min read

Key takeaways

  • When someone asks ChatGPT for a local business, it usually searches the web, reads a few pages and names the businesses those pages back up.
  • The searches it runs are often different from the question the customer typed. We use a tool that reveals those hidden searches, so we know which ones you need to rank for.
  • Clear service pages, identical business details everywhere and steady reviews do most of the work.
  • Structured data and an llms.txt file help machines read your site. They won't rescue a thin one.

A homeowner on Hamilton Mountain wakes up to a cold house. Instead of Googling, she opens ChatGPT and types: "Who should I call for furnace repair near me?"

ChatGPT names three companies. If you fix furnaces on the Mountain and you're not one of them, that call went somewhere else. You'll never even know it existed.

So how do you get mentioned in ChatGPT? Not with a trick. With the same groundwork that wins on Google, aimed a little differently.

How does ChatGPT decide which businesses to name?

For a question about a local service, ChatGPT usually doesn't answer from memory. It runs web searches, reads a handful of the pages that come back, and writes its answer from them.

Think of it like a new neighbour asking around. They check a few sources, notice which names come up more than once, and pass on the ones that sound reliable.

That gives you two jobs. Be findable in the searches ChatGPT runs. And make sure everything written about you agrees, so it has no reason to doubt you.

Nobody can sell you a place in those answers or guarantee one. What you can do is make your business the easiest one to find and the hardest one to get wrong.

Step 1: Find out what ChatGPT actually searches

Here's the part most people miss. ChatGPT doesn't search the customer's exact words.

A long question like "my furnace is banging and I'm in Stoney Creek, who's reliable?" might get broken into shorter searches, something like "furnace repair Stoney Creek" and "furnace banging noise". Those searches happen behind the scenes. The customer sees the answer, not the searches.

We use a tool we built that reveals those hidden searches. We run the questions your customers really ask, see what ChatGPT searches for each one, and then check whether your pages show up for those searches.

That turns a fuzzy goal, "get into ChatGPT", into a normal SEO job with a list attached: rank for these searches, with these pages. If you'd like to test your own business first, our guide to checking whether ChatGPT recommends your business walks you through it.

Step 2: Give every service its own clear page

AI tools quote sentences. If your website says "we offer a full range of plumbing services" and nothing else, there's nothing worth quoting.

Now picture a page about water heater replacement that says, in plain words, which types you install, which parts of Hamilton you cover, what affects the price and what happens after someone calls. That page answers the question. It can be lifted straight into a reply.

A few habits make the difference:

  • One service per page. "Drain cleaning" and "sump pump installation" are different questions, so they get different pages.
  • Say where you work, in sentences. "We repair furnaces and heat pumps across Stoney Creek, Winona and the rest of Hamilton's east end" is clearer than a list of postal codes.
  • Answer first, explain second. A sentence that makes sense on its own is easy to quote.
  • Use your real numbers. If you share price ranges, make them yours, and keep them current.

We cover the full page formula in how to write service pages that rank.

Step 3: Make your business details match everywhere

Your business name, phone number, address or service area, hours and website should read the same on your site, your Google Business Profile, Bing, Apple Maps and every directory you're listed in.

That sounds like housekeeping. To a machine trying to work out who you are, it's evidence.

In our own audits we've found two separate businesses sharing one phone number across their listings. A customer might never notice. A system cross-checking sources sees two companies claiming the same number, and has a reason to doubt both. Our article on NAP consistency and citations shows how to find and fix mismatches like that.

Step 4: Earn reviews and mentions beyond your own website

Your website is you talking about yourself. ChatGPT also reads what other people say about you.

That means Google reviews, directory listings, local news, supplier "where to buy" pages, trade association member lists and roundups of local businesses. When a Dundas landscaper shows up in several independent places, all describing the same business in the same area, that's a pattern an AI can trust.

Reviews matter most, and the honest way is the only way. Ask every happy customer, make it easy, and reply to every review. Google's rules ban paying for reviews or offering discounts in exchange for them. Our guide on how to get more Google reviews has the practical version.

Step 5: Remove anything an AI could repeat wrong

This is where a lot of local websites quietly hurt themselves.

When we scored 35 live local-business websites on Sep 20, 2026, the average was 34 out of 100. Some of the worst problems weren't missing things. They were wrong things that were still live:

  • Template text left over from another business, naming a different Ontario city on several service pages.
  • A fake phone number from a cloned template, sitting on 19 pages.
  • Impressive-sounding numbers on a homepage that nothing backed up.

A person might skim past those. An AI reading your site might repeat them to a customer as fact. Before you work on getting mentioned, make sure what gets mentioned is true.

Step 6: Help machines read you

On the sites we build, we add structured data and an llms.txt file from day one.

Structured data is a small block of code that states your business facts in a format machines understand: name, phone, service area, hours and services. An llms.txt file is a plain-text summary of your site, written for AI tools.

Both help a machine read you correctly. Neither will make a thin website worth recommending. We explain what schema markup does for AI search, and what it doesn't, in a separate article.

What doesn't work

  • Stuffing pages with "best plumber in Hamilton". Repeating a phrase doesn't make it true, and it reads badly to people and machines alike.
  • Fake or bought reviews. They break Google's rules and can be removed, taking the trust they were meant to build with them.
  • Anyone selling a guaranteed mention. Nobody outside the company that runs the AI controls what it says.
  • Ignoring Google. The groundwork that gets you into ChatGPT is mostly the same groundwork that gets you into the map pack and the regular results.

FAQ

How long does it take to get mentioned in ChatGPT?

There's no fixed timeline. It depends on how quickly the pages ChatGPT searches get updated and re-read. Fixes to your listings and service pages can start to show within weeks. Building enough reviews and outside mentions usually takes months.

Can I pay to be recommended by ChatGPT?

No agency can sell you a place in ChatGPT's answers or guarantee one. What you can control is how easy your business is to find, how clearly your pages answer questions and how consistent your details are across the web.

Does ChatGPT just copy Google's top results?

No. It runs its own searches and reads the pages it finds. But the work that helps you rank on Google, clear pages, good reviews and consistent details, also helps ChatGPT find and trust you.

Will ChatGPT give every customer the same answer?

Not always. Answers change with the wording of the question, the person's location and settings, and sometimes from one try to the next. That's why we test many versions of the same question, not just one.
