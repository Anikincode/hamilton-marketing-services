How diners search

Hungry now, or planning for Saturday?

Restaurant search moves faster than almost any other kind. Someone walking along James Street North decides where to eat in a minute or two, often without ever opening a website.

Right-now searches

On a phone, close by, ready to walk in. The map, your photos, your hours and your star rating decide it.

  • "restaurants near me"
  • "lunch near McMaster"
  • "pizza Locke Street"
  • "late night food downtown Hamilton"

Planned searches

Date nights, birthdays, group dinners, catering. These people look at the menu, check prices and want to book.

  • "best brunch Hamilton"
  • "patio restaurant Dundas"
  • "private dining room Ancaster"
  • "catering Stoney Creek"

The big difference from the trades: you're competing with every restaurant within a short walk or drive, and you're judged mostly on the Business Profile. A plumber can win with a great website. A restaurant wins or loses on the profile first.

Google Business Profile

Be specific about what you serve

Google's advice is to choose categories that describe your business as specifically as possible. "Restaurant" alone tells it very little.

Lead with the cuisine or format people search for, then add secondaries that are true:

  • A cuisine or format as primary. For example "Italian restaurant", "Brunch restaurant", "Pizza restaurant" or "Sushi restaurant".
  • Bar, Cocktail bar or Wine bar. If the bar is a real draw on its own.
  • Caterer. Only if you take catering orders regularly.
  • Takeout Restaurant or Event venue. When those are real parts of the business.

Google's list is long and changes over time, so search it for the closest fit during setup.

Fill in everything a diner checks

  • Hours, plus special hours for holidays and patio season.
  • A menu link that opens a readable page, not a blurry photo.
  • Reservation or ordering links, if you take them.
  • Fresh photos of real dishes and the room.
  • Attributes like patio, vegetarian options and accessibility, where true.

Reviews and posts

Reviews are the menu people read first

For a restaurant, reviews are constant and public. Diners read the recent ones for the food, the service and whether it's worth the wait.

You don't need to ask every table, but a small card with the bill or a QR code by the door helps. Treat every table alike. Google's rules don't allow rewards for reviews or filtering so only happy guests are asked.

Reply to reviews in your own voice. Thank people by name for specifics, and answer complaints without getting defensive. Future guests are reading your replies as much as the reviews.

Posts that follow the city's calendar

Hamilton gives restaurants plenty to post about. A James Street North spot can post for the monthly art crawl and Supercrawl. A Dundas place can post around the Cactus Festival, a Stoney Creek one around the Winona Peach Festival.

Post new dishes, patio openings and holiday menus too. Here's how to write Business Profile posts people actually read.

Website and AI search

A website that Google and AI can read

When someone asks ChatGPT or Google's AI Overviews for "the best brunch in Hamilton", the answer is pulled from websites, listings and reviews. If your menu is a photo, it can't be quoted.

Menu as text

A real web page with dishes and prices, not a PDF or an image. It loads faster on a phone and search engines can read every line.

Structured data

Restaurant schema tells search engines your cuisine, hours, address and menu in a format they understand. See schema markup for AI search.

Pages for what you're known for

Brunch, private events, catering, a signature dish. If people search for it, it deserves a page of its own.

Ads, seasons and common problems

What else matters for a Hamilton restaurant

A few practical points that come up every time.

  • No Local Services Ads. In Canada they're only for the trades, so restaurants can't use them. Regular search ads can help for catering or private events, where one booking is worth a lot.
  • Seasons. Patio season, holiday parties and McMaster and Mohawk students coming back each fall all shift what people search. Update hours, posts and menu pages before each one.
  • Conflicting numbers. We've seen a website show three different review counts on the same site, from old widgets and badges. It makes diners wonder which one is real. Pick one source and keep it current.
  • Old hours online. Nothing loses a regular faster than turning up to a locked door because the website still shows summer hours.

FAQ

Restaurant SEO questions

Is a PDF menu bad for SEO?

It's not ideal. PDFs are slow on phones and harder for search engines and AI tools to use. A simple HTML menu page is better for diners and for search. Keep the PDF as a download if you like it.

Which primary category should my restaurant choose?

The most specific one that's true and that people search for. If you're known for brunch, "Brunch restaurant" may beat a cuisine category. Test changes one at a time and watch your results.

Do I need a website if I'm on delivery apps?

Yes. Delivery apps own their pages, not you. Your own site is where you control the menu, the story, private events and catering, and it's what Google and AI tools read to understand your restaurant.

How do I show up for "near me" searches downtown?

Distance is a big factor, so you can't move closer. But you can be more relevant and more prominent: a complete profile, a specific category, a readable menu, fresh photos and a steady flow of reviews.

Can restaurants use Local Services Ads?

Not in Canada. They're limited to trade categories here. Restaurants rely on the Business Profile, organic search and, where it makes sense, regular Google Ads.
