Pricing guide

SEO content writing cost: per page, per word or retainer?

Paying for content by the word is like paying a plumber by the metre of pipe. You'll get plenty of pipe. Here's how content is priced, and what's actually worth paying for.

Kirill Anikin · October 30, 2026 · 5 min read

Key takeaways

  • SEO content is priced per word, per page or as a monthly retainer. Per page usually fits service businesses best.
  • Most of the cost of a good page is research and interviewing you, not typing.
  • Write service pages first. They bring calls. Blog posts support them later.
  • Cheap content tends to be generic or copied from templates, which can do more harm than good.

A plumber paid by the metre of pipe would run pipe through every room. A writer paid by the word will write a lot of words.

Length isn't what makes a page rank. Usefulness is. So how you pay for content shapes what you get. Here are the common pricing models, what drives the cost and what you should expect for your money.

Three ways content is priced

Per word

A rate for every word written. Easy to compare between writers.

The catch: it rewards length over usefulness. A page that answers "how much does drain cleaning cost in Dundas" in one clear paragraph is worth more than three padded screens.

Per page

A fixed price for a finished page: research, writing and on-page setup. This fits service businesses well, because every service page has one clear job.

The catch: check what "finished" means. Does it include the title, description, headings and links to your other pages, or just the text?

Monthly retainer

A set amount of content each month, plus updates to existing pages. It fits a business adding services, areas or seasonal pages over time.

The catch: make sure the plan follows a page map, not a quota. Publishing for the sake of it creates pages that compete with each other.

What drives the cost of a page

The writing is the quick part. Most of the cost is what happens before and after it.

  1. Research. Which search is this page for? What else do people search alongside it? Which questions show up in Google's "People also ask" boxes?
  2. Interviewing you. The facts that make a page worth reading only come from you: how you price, what you see on jobs, how quickly you can reach Waterdown, what customers always ask.
  3. Writing. Plain English, short paragraphs, one clear purpose.
  4. On-page setup. Title, description, main heading, structured data and links to your other pages.
  5. Your review. You check the facts before anything goes live.

Cheap content skips step two. That's why it reads like it could be about any company in any city. SEO people call the opposite "information gain": a page that adds something the other results don't. Our article on information gain explains why it matters.

Service pages first, then blog posts

If your budget is limited, spend it on service pages. A page for "water heater installation Hamilton" can bring in a call the week it starts ranking. A blog post on "how long does a water heater last" is useful, but it's a step further from the phone.

Blog posts earn their place once your services are covered, by answering questions and linking back to the pages that sell. Our guide to service pages that rank shows what a good one includes.

Don't forget updates: often the cheapest win

New pages get all the attention. Updating pages you already have often costs less and pays back sooner.

Here's why. A page sitting at position 4 to 10 on Google is close. Sometimes all it needs is a better title or a clearer opening. We've seen it on our own site: a page at average position 3 to 5 with zero clicks from 233 impressions. The page ranked. Nobody clicked, because the title gave them no reason to.

So when you compare content prices, ask whether updates are included, and how they're priced. A plan that rewrites the titles on your "furnace repair Hamilton" and "AC installation Hamilton" pages, then checks the clicks again after 30 days, can be worth more than another new blog post.

That monthly title check is part of the Core 30 method we work to, alongside writing new pages.

AI-written content and the price

AI tools have made words cheap. They haven't made useful pages cheap. Google's position is that it cares whether content is helpful, not how it was produced, but mass-produced pages made to game rankings count as spam.

The bigger risk is careless copying. We've found a paragraph naming a different Ontario city on several service pages of one site, and a project page whose body came from a template for a company in another country. Nobody had read them before they went live.

What you should get for the money

  • One primary search per page, and no two pages chasing the same one
  • Your real facts and real examples, plus prices or price ranges if you're comfortable sharing them
  • Answers to the questions customers actually ask
  • A title, description and main heading written for that search
  • Links to related pages on your site
  • Your approval before it goes live
  • Full ownership of the words once you've paid

A worked example: when does a page pay for itself?

Say a "furnace installation Ancaster" page costs $P to write. If one installation is worth $X in profit to you, the page has paid for itself after P ÷ X jobs.

And unlike an ad, the page doesn't stop working when the invoice is paid. If it keeps ranking, every job after that is profit. That's why a well-researched page that costs more can beat a cheap page that never ranks at all.

Common questions about content writing cost

How long should a service page be?

Long enough to answer what your customers ask, and no longer. There's no magic word count. A clear page that answers real questions beats a long one that pads.

Can I write my own content?

Yes, and your knowledge is the most valuable part. A good middle path is to write a rough draft, or answer questions in a voice note, then have it edited for search. That cuts the cost and keeps it in your words.

Is blog content worth paying for?

Once your service pages are done, yes, if each post answers a real question and links to a service page. Before that, service pages come first. See does a local business need a blog?

Who owns the content I pay for?

You should. Make sure the agreement says so, so you can keep the pages if you ever change companies.
