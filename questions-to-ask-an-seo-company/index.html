Buyer guide

15 questions to ask an SEO company before you sign

Bring this list to every sales call, including ours. A good SEO company will be glad you asked. A bad one will change the subject.

Kirill Anikin · October 30, 2026 · 5 min read

Key takeaways

  • Ask what they'd fix first on your site. A good company answers with specifics, not a package.
  • Ask how they measure success. The answer should be calls and leads, not traffic estimates or keyword counts.
  • A guarantee of first-page rankings is a reason to leave. Google says nobody can promise that.
  • Make sure every account, from your Business Profile to your website, stays in your name.

Plenty of business owners hire an SEO company after one sales call and a nice slide deck. Then they spend the next year wondering what they're paying for.

These questions fix that. Ask the same ones of every company you talk to, write down the answers and compare. The differences show up fast.

Questions about the work

1. What would you fix first on my site, and why?

A good answer: something specific to your business. "Your Business Profile's primary category is wrong, so you're missing searches for your main service." That means they actually looked.

Watch out for: a package read off a menu before anyone has seen your site.

2. Which searches will you target, and how did you choose them?

A good answer: searches that lead to calls, grouped by what the searcher wants. "Furnace repair Stoney Creek" is an emergency. "Furnace replacement cost" is research. They need different pages.

Watch out for: a list of hundreds of keywords, or terms nobody in Hamilton searches.

3. What will you do with my Google Business Profile?

A good answer: categories, services, photos, reviews and posts. For most local businesses, this profile decides whether you show on Maps at all. Our Business Profile optimization page shows what that work covers.

Watch out for: advice to stuff keywords into your business name or list an address you don't really work from. Google's guidelines ban both, and breaking them can get a profile suspended.

4. Will you write new pages, and who writes them?

A good answer: one page per service, written after asking you how you actually do the work. You approve everything before it goes live.

Watch out for: templates with the city name swapped. We've found a fake phone number from a cloned template sitting on 19 pages of one site.

5. How do you get links to my site?

A good answer: real mentions from real places: suppliers, trade associations, local sponsorships, sites your customers actually read. Slow, but safe.

Watch out for: a set number of backlinks a month, sold like a subscription. We once had to build a disavow file for a small business site with 60 spam domains pointing links at it.

6. What happens to my rankings if my site changes?

A good answer: every old page address gets mapped to a new one and redirected before anything goes live.

Watch out for: a shrug. We've seen pages deleted with no redirect, including one that ranked for a search with real monthly volume.

Questions about results

7. Can you guarantee first-page rankings?

A good answer: no. Google's own guidance on hiring an SEO says nobody can guarantee a #1 ranking.

Watch out for: yes. Either it's a search nobody makes, or it's a promise they can't keep.

8. How will you measure whether it's working?

A good answer: calls, form leads, clicks from Google Search Console and map rankings across your service area.

Watch out for: traffic estimates from keyword tools. On one site, a tool estimated traffic about five times higher than Search Console showed.

9. How do you track rankings across my service area?

A good answer: from many points across the area, not one. The map pack changes with where the searcher is standing, so someone on Upper James sees different results from someone in Dundas. This is called geo-grid rank tracking.

Watch out for: one screenshot, taken from the agency's own office.

10. How long before I see results?

A good answer: an honest range. Quick fixes, like a corrected profile or a better title, can show within weeks. New pages and new rankings usually take months.

Watch out for: "page one by next month".

Questions about working together

11. Who owns my accounts and content?

A good answer: you do. Your Business Profile, Search Console, analytics, ad account, website, domain and every page written for you.

Watch out for: accounts set up under the agency's login, or a website you can't take with you.

12. How long is the contract, and how do I leave?

A good answer: clear terms in writing, with a simple way out.

Watch out for: long lock-ins and cancellation fees buried in the fine print.

13. Who will actually do the work?

A good answer: a name, plus an honest answer about whether any of the work is passed to subcontractors.

Watch out for: the person who sold you the plan vanishing the day you sign.

14. What will the monthly report show?

A good answer: what was done, what changed, what didn't work and what's next, in plain English. Our guide to what an SEO report should show has the details.

Watch out for: pages of automatic charts that nobody explains.

15. Do you understand my trade and my area?

A good answer: they know how your customers search. An emergency plumber and a kitchen designer get very different searches. They should know the city too: that the Mountain and the lower city behave like different markets, and that people in Ancaster often search "Ancaster" or "near me", not "Hamilton".

Watch out for: a pitch that would work word for word for a business in any city.

How to use the answers

Put the answers side by side. The cheapest company isn't always the worst, and the most polished isn't always the best. Look for the one that answered with specifics about your business.

Common questions about vetting an SEO company

How many SEO companies should I talk to?

A few is enough. Too many and the answers blur together. Ask each one the same questions, so you're comparing like with like.

Is it a red flag if they ask for access to my accounts?

No. They need access to do the work. The red flag is when they ask you to hand over ownership instead of adding them as a user.

Should I ask for references?

Yes, and for examples of similar work. Ask what was done, not just what improved. A company that can't explain its own results probably can't repeat them.

What if a company won't answer some of these?

That's an answer too. Some details are fair to keep private, like exactly how they price their tools. But a company that dodges guarantees, contracts or ownership is telling you something.
