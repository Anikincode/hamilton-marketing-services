Website redesign SEO: how to rebuild without losing your rankings

A new website should bring in more calls, not fewer. Here's how to move house without Google losing your address.

Kirill Anikin · October 30, 2026 · 6 min read

Key takeaways

  • Google ranks pages at addresses. Change the addresses or delete the pages, and the rankings attached to them can disappear.
  • Before anything is designed, list every page that gets traffic and the searches it ranks for.
  • Every old URL needs a permanent redirect to its closest new page. Not everything to the homepage.
  • On launch day, check that the new site isn't blocking Google and isn't carrying demo text from its template.

Moving your shop from Ottawa Street to Locke Street? You'd forward the mail, change the sign and tell your regulars where you went.

A website redesign is the same kind of move. Google is the regular customer, and your page addresses are where it expects to find you. Skip the forwarding and it shows up at an empty building.

This is where most redesigns go wrong. The new site looks better, launches on time, and the phone goes quiet a few weeks later. Here's how to avoid that.

Why redesigns lose rankings

Google doesn't rank "your business". It ranks individual pages, each at its own web address, or URL.

Your "furnace repair Ancaster" page may have spent years earning its spot. It has links pointing at it, a title Google trusts and content that matches the search. A redesign can break all three at once: a new URL, a new title and rewritten text.

To Google, that's not your old page with a fresh coat of paint. It's a brand-new page with no history, competing from scratch.

Step 1: Take an inventory before anyone designs anything

Before a single mock-up, write down what you already have. You can't protect what you haven't listed.

  • Every URL on the current site. Your sitemap or a crawler gives you the full list.
  • Which pages bring in visitors. In Search Console's Performance report, sort pages by clicks and by impressions.
  • What each one ranks for. Click a page and look at its queries. Write down the main two or three.
  • Which pages bring in calls or quote requests. These are the ones you can least afford to lose.

A Waterdown landscaping company might find that one old page about interlock repair quietly brings in more quote requests than the homepage. That page gets protected, whatever the new design looks like.

Step 2: Build a redirect map

A redirect map is a two-column list: old URL on the left, new URL on the right. Every old page that matters gets a permanent (301) redirect to the closest matching new page.

The temptation is to redirect everything to the homepage. Don't. If someone clicks an old link to your "basement waterproofing" page, they should land on your new basement waterproofing page, not a homepage they have to dig through.

We've seen what happens without a map. In one audit, pages had been deleted with no redirect at all, including one that ranked for a search with real monthly volume. That ranking didn't move to the new site. It simply vanished.

Google's own guidance on moving a site with URL changes says the same: map old URLs to new ones and use permanent redirects.

Step 3: Keep what's working on the pages that rank

A redesign is a design project. It doesn't have to be a rewrite of every page at once.

If a page ranks well, keep its main topic, its title and the core of its content. Improve it, by all means. But change one big thing at a time, so that if rankings move you know why.

Watch for new pages that compete with old ones too. We saw a new page take over six of a homepage's searches, and all six dropped. A redesign is the perfect moment to create that problem by accident, so check every new page against the rule of one page, one keyword.

Step 4: Clear out the template's leftovers

New sites are often built from templates, and templates come with someone else's words in them.

In our audits we've found:

  • A fake phone number from a cloned template sitting on 19 pages.
  • A project page whose body was copied from a template for a company in another country.
  • A paragraph naming a different Ontario city repeated across several service pages.

None of that is obvious when you look at the homepage. Before launch, search the whole new site for the template's demo phone number, placeholder names and any city you don't serve. If you work in Stoney Creek and the word "Oakville" shows up on your site, find out why.

Step 5: The launch-day checklist

Launch day is when the costly mistakes happen. Go through this list the same day, not the following week.

  1. Google can get in. Sites are often hidden from search while they're being built. We've audited a live site whose robots.txt still blocked all indexing. Read the robots.txt mistakes we see most and check yours.
  2. No stray "noindex" tags. Check the pages that matter most, not just the homepage.
  3. Redirects work. Click through a sample of old URLs and make sure each lands on the right new page.
  4. Search Console still works. If verification lived in the old site's code, it may need redoing. Submit the new sitemap.
  5. Phone number and forms work on a phone. Tap the number. Send a test quote request.
  6. Speed on mobile. Run the main pages through PageSpeed Insights. Big new photos are the usual culprit.

Step 6: Watch closely for the first few weeks

Some movement after a launch is normal while Google recrawls everything. What you're watching for is movement that doesn't recover.

Check Search Console's Pages report for new "Not found (404)" errors, which point to redirects you missed. Compare clicks on your key pages with your inventory from Step 1. If a page that used to bring in calls has gone quiet, look at it first.

This is a big part of what we call defensive SEO: protecting rankings you already earned from deleted pages, broken redirects and careless edits. We explain the idea in defensive SEO for local businesses.

Changing platforms at the same time?

Moving from one website builder to another nearly always changes your URLs, because each platform formats addresses its own way. That makes the redirect map even more important.

Some platforms make redirects easy and some hide them in settings menus. Wix, for example, has a redirect manager built in. If you're on Wix or moving to it, our page on Wix SEO covers its traps.

If you can, avoid changing the platform, the domain and all the content at once. Every extra change makes it harder to tell what caused a drop.

Frequently asked questions

Will I lose rankings when I redesign my website?

Not if it's planned. Some short-term movement is common while Google recrawls. Lasting losses usually come from missing redirects, deleted content, changed titles or a site left blocked from search.

Can I just redirect all my old pages to the homepage?

You can, but you shouldn't. Each old page should point to its closest new match. Sending everything to the homepage throws away what each page was ranking for.

How long should I keep the redirects?

For as long as you can, ideally for good. They cost nothing to keep, and old links from other websites, directories and bookmarks keep working.

Should the SEO person be involved before the designer starts?

Yes. Page structure, URLs and titles are decided early in a design project. Fixing them after launch costs more and loses time. It's one reason many owners use one team for SEO and web design.
